# Compute Fox 3 reach from open physics

A Fox 3 is an air-to-air missile whose terminal seeker is an active radar, and the famous fielded rounds are the AIM-54 Phoenix, the AIM-120 AMRAAM series, MBDA Meteor and RF MICA, and the R-77 and R-37 families, with Rafael I-Derby ER, TÜBİTAK SAGE Gökdoğan, and DRDO Astra Mk-1 the only other rounds whose opened manufacturer or government pages state both an active radio-frequency seeker and a midcourse path. China’s PL-12 and PL-15, Japan’s AAM-4, and a string of follow-on names are described the same way in secondary writing, but those pages do not support the same card. The families split on support and on thrust, not on a kilometre: Phoenix keeps semi-active midcourse and only then goes active, while AMRAAM, Meteor, MICA, the R-77, and the R-37 fly an inertial midcourse on a datalink; Meteor is a throttleable solid-fuel ducted rocket that MBDA says is still thrusting at intercept, and the others are solid rockets whose “dual-pulse,” “dual-thrust,” and “dual-mode” labels are three different curves, none of them published. Pitbull, in the March 2023 brevity manual, means an active missile such as the AIM-120 is at medium-PRF range, and the definition prints no distance. missilesim should store mass, diameter, fin type, and those guidance and motor flags, leave thrust, burn time, seeker band, handover range, and lethal radius empty wherever a page did not print them, and let loft, detection, and miss come out of the integrator. The live hit test is still a swept sphere of target radius plus a proximity radius that defaults to **18 m**, and that sphere does not ask what the seeker is tracking ([PhysicsEngine.cpp](C:/Users/alede/Documents/code/missilesim/src/physics/PhysicsEngine.cpp)).

## Pitbull is a pulse-rate call, not a distance

The March 2023 multi-service brevity manual defines FOX THREE as launch of an active radar-guided missile, FOX ONE as semi-active, and FOX TWO as infrared. PITBULL is “an active radar guided missile (e.g., AIM-120) is at MPRF active range.” HUSKY is the same missile at high-PRF active range. CHEAPSHOT is the datalink ending between those two PRF states. None of the three entries gives nautical miles, seconds, or a radar cross section ([ATP 1-02.1, March 2023](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/83/ALSSA_Brevity.pdf)). NAVAIR’s AMRAAM page says the missile flies inertially, takes datalink updates from the launch aircraft, and “transitions to a self-guiding terminal mode when the target is within range of its own monopulse radar set,” with a home-on-jam mode and an active-radar proximity fuze. “Within range” is not a number ([NAVAIR AMRAAM](https://www.navair.navy.mil/product/AMRAAM)). A stored pitbull of 10 nm, or of any other fixed gate, invents a distance the manual does not contain.

Skolnik’s seeker chapter is why that gate cannot be repaired by picking a round number. Active homing is a self-contained radar: the missile transmits and tracks the reflection. A comparable semi-active seeker, fed by a large illuminator, outranges it, because the missile’s antenna and transmitter are small, so active-all-the-way is a short-range mode. The long-range pattern is an inertial or command midcourse that delivers the missile inside terminal range, “typically the last 10 guidance time constants or a few kilometers from intercept,” after which the seeker searches the target uncertainty and only then homes. The same chapter later puts later-generation homing at “only the last few seconds of flight (typically 10 guidance time constants).” Those two scales agree only for one closing speed and one time constant. They are a warning about remaining homing time, not an acquisition range and not a card constant ([Skolnik, *Radar Handbook*, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)). The AIM-120 encyclopedia article, which is a secondary description, says the aircraft passes location, direction, and speed, updates change direction and speed, and the fins steer until the target lies in the basket, defined there as the missile radar’s field of view. At short range the active seeker can be used just after launch. The article prints neither a range nor a time-to-go for transmitter turn-on ([AIM-120 AMRAAM, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-120_AMRAAM)).

The flight is four guidance phases with the motor held as a separate state. Launch inherits the shooter’s velocity. A rail holds that direction until a clearance time, because an opened separation description is a constrained slide and then tip-off, not an instant free body. An eject shot adds a velocity in aircraft axes and then the same clearance. No universal fighter eject speed was published, so the default increment is zero and stays labeled unpublished ([Anandhanarayanan et al., abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274433064_Separation_Dynamics_of_Air-to-Air_Missile_Using_a_Grid-Free_Euler_Solver)). Until the seeker has acquired, the line-of-sight rate in Zarchan’s command is not the true target. The missile integrates its own state and a target state from pre-launch data, replaced when an update arrives, and steers toward the predicted intercept. Search starts only when that prediction lies in the angle, range, and Doppler basket the seeker can actually search, and enough homing time remains. Terminal proportional navigation starts after acquisition, and the datalink is no longer required. Coast, after burnout or between pulses, is a propulsion state. It does not by itself switch the seeker or the law ([Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf); [Zarchan, *Tactical and Strategic Missile Guidance* (2012), ch. 2](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)).

### Loft is an altitude program, and pure proportional navigation will not climb

Zero line-of-sight rate is a collision triangle, so \(n_c = N' V_c \dot\lambda\) commands nothing. A missile placed on that triangle and then flown with proportional navigation from launch does not loft. The climb has to be commanded by something else. Berglund’s NATO lecture states the reason air-to-air missiles optimise the midcourse at all: lower drag in thinner air, and the objectives he lists — minimum time of flight, maximum range, maximum terminal velocity — are different problems, not one knob ([Berglund, in RTO-EN-018](https://dn720709.ca.archive.org/0/items/technologies-for-future-precision-strike-ada-394520/Technologies%20for%20Future%20Precision%20Strike%20%28ADA394520%29.pdf)). The opened air-to-air law that actually flies the climb is not proportional navigation. For a predicted intercept, the trajectory that maximises terminal speed is a climb and then a pull-down, tracked by augmented pursuit \(a_{\mathrm{com}} = N V (\lambda_{\mathrm{com}} - \lambda)\), with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{com}}\) chosen so altitude is a polynomial of range-to-go. A higher peak leaves less acceleration margin when the predicted intercept moves, because the pull-down is already spending angle of attack in thin air ([Shin, Tahk, Kim, and Lee, ICAS 2024](https://www.icas.org/icas_archive/icas2024/data/papers/icas2024_0828_paper.pdf)). Drag is \(D = C_D \rho V^2 A / 2\), so dynamic pressure \(q = \rho V^2/2\) is the term a climb is trying to cut ([NASA Glenn, drag equation](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/drag-equation/)). Terminal speed is higher on the loft only when the drag work saved exceeds the drag of the extra path and any specific energy still sitting in altitude at intercept. Time of flight is scored separately. Zarchan’s ballistic closed form says a higher flight-path angle takes longer, and that form is a coasting arc, not an air-to-air drag solution; a motor that is still thrusting can break it ([Zarchan (2012)](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)).

Zarchan’s trajectory-shaping command is a different requirement: hit, and arrive on a chosen final line-of-sight angle, while minimising the integral of acceleration squared. The form the opened text states is \(n_c = 4 V_c \dot\lambda + 2 V_c (\lambda - \lambda_F)/t_{go} + n_T\). It spends more acceleration than proportional navigation. It is not the proof that the climb saves drag ([Zarchan (2012), ch. 24](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)). Licata’s cruise band of about **500–1000 psf**, a post-burnout glide near **700 psf**, and the warning that a climb thrust-to-weight above about **2** raises dynamic pressure, sit in the same NATO volume as design guidelines for a precision-strike missile. They are not an AMRAAM or Meteor card ([Licata, in RTO-EN-018](https://dn720709.ca.archive.org/0/items/technologies-for-future-precision-strike-ada-394520/Technologies%20for%20Future%20Precision%20Strike%20%28ADA394520%29.pdf)). No opened air-to-air page prints the polynomial coefficients, the cruise altitude, or the dynamic-pressure band of a named Fox 3. The default loft in this sim is the ICAS altitude polynomial of predicted range-to-go, tracked by a lead angle, and it switches off when search starts. The \(\lambda_F\) law is a separate card option. Pure proportional navigation for the whole flight is the third option, and it is the collision-triangle test. They are not blended in one tick.

The uplink that belongs on a Fox 3 is a target state, not a fin command. Skolnik also describes command guidance, in which the missile does not see the target and the uplink is a steering order. That architecture is a different weapon. Between messages the missile coasts the last target position at the last target velocity and with acceleration zero, unless the message actually carried an acceleration. If the true target acceleration perpendicular to the predicted line of sight is a constant \(n_T\) that the message did not contain, Zarchan’s predicted-intercept term says the position error after an update age \(\tau\) is \(\tfrac{1}{2} n_T \tau^2\). That identification uses the age of the update, not time-to-go. It is an inference from the equation he prints, not a sentence in an AIM-120 interface document. Augmented proportional navigation adds \(0.5 N' n_T\) only when a message or a filter really estimates \(n_T\). A direction-and-speed update, which is what the AIM-120 article describes, does not ([Zarchan (2012), ch. 2 and ch. 8](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt); [AIM-120 AMRAAM, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM-120_AMRAAM)). The look angle of that error is \(\mathrm{atan2}(\tfrac{1}{2} n_T \tau^2, R)\). If it falls outside the field the seeker will search, acquisition fails even when a hard-coded range says the missile is close enough. No opened source gives the message rate in hertz. Skolnik only says midcourse can run slower than terminal homing.

### Miss is the separation when closing speed changes sign

After lock, the guidance law does not care that the angle came from monopulse rather than an infrared centroid. The command the sensor model already has to consume is \(n_c = N' V_c \dot\lambda\), perpendicular to the line of sight, with \(N'\) usually **3 to 5** and \(V_c\) the closing speed. One lag, \(\dot n = (n_c - n)/T\), is the smallest autopilot that reproduces Zarchan’s adjoint. Available load factor is the minimum of the structural cap and \(q S C_{N,\max}/m\). Miss distance is the separation at the sign change of closing velocity. It is not a scripted penalty and not a sphere ([Zarchan (2012)](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)). The Fox 2 path in this project instead commands \(N\) times missile speed, perpendicular to velocity, with \(N\) clamped to 3–5, and blends a heading-error turn. That law is not the one Zarchan linearizes. Putting it on an active terminal phase makes the gate \(\dot\lambda = 0 \Rightarrow n_c = 0\) ambiguous whenever closing speed and missile speed differ. Fox 3 terminal homing uses the closing-speed form. The **0.25 s** lag printed for the AIM-9B is that round’s, and it is not copied onto a radar missile. \(T\) stays a card field. No opened Fox 3 page prints \(N'\) or \(T\).

Home-on-jam is a change of angle source, not of law. Skolnik: when jamming makes skin track impossible, the seeker passively tracks the jammer; when the jammer-to-signal ratio falls enough that skin track works again, skin is preferred. He prints no numeric threshold. The criterion he states is which mode gives the better guidance information ([Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)). The mode waits on the jamming model. Until that model exists it is a flag, unused. A lofted terminal dive looks down, and a noncoherent pulse seeker was, in Skolnik’s account, unsuitable against small aircraft in low-altitude clutter, which is why air-defence active seekers use pulse-Doppler. Clutter is still a competing cross section in the same dwell. It is not a second Fox 3 detection law and not a range debit stored on the card.

The motor uses the force law the integrator already has — sea-level momentum thrust plus a nozzle term \((P_e - P_a)A_e\), drag from dynamic pressure — and four state machines. Boost-coast is one ignition and a thrust table, then zero thrust. Boost-sustain is the same ignition with a high segment and a low segment and no zero between them, and no second igniter. Dual-pulse is two ignitions, a coast at thrust near zero, and a predicate for the second burn. A patent claims one predicate, conserve the second charge until the vehicle’s own sensors acquire and then light it on the turn to the homing heading, and it gives no thrust split ([US20040124306A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20040124306A1/en)). A journal abstract says the delay between pulses is choosable and says nothing about a flight curve ([Li, Wang, and others, *Acta Astronautica*, 2018, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094576517309864)). That patent predicate is not the default for every dual-pulse round. A time delay is the other predicate the opened propulsion texts support. Range, dynamic pressure, and time-to-go are not published triggers. A throttleable ducted rocket is a booster, then a valve-controlled gas generator whose thrust also depends on captured air, so on altitude and Mach. Specific impulse in the solid-rocket sense, \(I_{sp} = F/(\dot m g_0)\), converts a rocket segment into mass flow only while that definition is meaningful. It does not convert a ramjet plume. A rectangular spread of impulse over burn time is legitimate only when a page printed both the impulse and the duration. For the Fox 3s below, those pairs were not printed, so thrust stays null. Back-solving a brochure range to fill the null is the failure this catalog exists to prevent.

Three checks close without a brochure. On a collision triangle, with gravity off or shared so relative acceleration is zero, the command is zero for the whole flight and the miss at the sign change of \(V_c\) is zero to integrator tolerance. A loft against that straight path, same motor and same end altitude, is scored by time of flight and by \(\int q S C_D\,\mathrm{d}s\) separately; the test fails if every climb is reported as both longer and faster, or if either number is read from a stored range. The nozzle term means the thrust impulse is the same across the two paths only when the altitude history is included. A datalink blackout with constant unsent \(n_T\) must grow the lateral error as \(\tfrac{1}{2} n_T \tau^2\) and must refuse the contact when that error walks outside the search field, even if the true target would have cleared a signal-to-noise test. The physics step is already **0.01 s** ([Application.h](C:/Users/alede/Documents/code/missilesim/src/application/Application.h)).

## Phoenix still needs illumination that AMRAAM drops

The AIM-54 is a Fox 3 because every opened official description gives it an active terminal seeker, and it is not an AIM-120. NAVAIR’s guidance line for the family is “semi-active and active radar homing.” The A is “semi-active, update and active radar.” The C is “semi-active, update, inertial and active radar” ([NAVAIR Phoenix, 5 Oct 2004](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/12701)). Long-range midcourse still depends on the fighter’s radar. Parsch writes that the AWG-9 periodically illuminates each target. That sentence is not on the NAVAIR sheet, so continuous illumination is not stored as a fact-sheet requirement. The same secondary page, citing Friedman, Jane’s, Gunston, and Chant, puts active homing on the final **18,200 m (20,000 yards)** and the minimum engagement at about **3.7 km (2 nm)**, and it flags those figures as unreliable. They are not a lock range ([designation-systems.net AIM-54](https://www.designation-systems.net/dusrm/m-54.html)). Home-on-jam is not stated for Phoenix on the pages opened here. ECCM on the C and on the ECCM/Sealed round is a different sentence. A loft appears only as Parsch’s “optimized high-altitude trajectory for reduced drag,” with no altitude and no angle. The guidance law is allowed to climb. **80,000–100,000 ft** was not on any page opened here and is not a ceiling to hard-code. The June 1973 shot against a BQM-34E at **110 nautical miles** is a test record on NAVAIR’s chronology, not a maximum range.

### One Phoenix fact sheet contradicts itself on mass, warhead, and range

NAVAIR’s general-characteristics block, one set of numbers for the family, gives length **13 feet (3.9 m)**, weight **1,024 pounds (460.8 kg)**, diameter **15 inches (38.1 cm)**, wing span **3 feet (0.9 m)**, range **in excess of 100 nautical miles**, and speed **in excess of 3,000 mph**. The pound and kilogram pair do not match. The same page’s AIM-54A block gives length **3.96 m**, diameter **380 mm**, wingspan **0.92 m**, launch weight **443 kg**, warhead **60 kg HE continuous rod**, fuze **IR**, and range **135 km**, and the prose says a design range of **60 nm** was surpassed in testing. The general block’s warhead is “proximity fuse, high explosive” at **135 pounds (60.75 kg)**. The FAS republication of a June 1997 Navy training-system plan says **1,000 pounds** for the A, hydraulically operated fins, and that the A, the C, and the ECCM/Sealed round then in service all use the **Mk 47 Mod 1** motor. It does not print a thrust curve. Parsch’s secondary table, which he flags as not reliable, gives **453 kg**, Mach **4.3**, range **130 km (72.5 nm)**, and a **60 kg MK 82 blast-fragmentation** warhead with radar proximity, infrared proximity, and impact. The mass and warhead lines disagree with NAVAIR and with each other. They are stored as sourced conflicts, not averaged ([NAVAIR Phoenix](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/12701); [FAS, citing N88-NTSP-A-50-8007C/D](https://web.archive.org/web/20160304112626/http://fas.org/man/dod-101/sys/missile/aim-54.htm); [Parsch](https://www.designation-systems.net/dusrm/m-54.html)). Burn time, thrust, and specific impulse for the Mk 47, and the Mk 60 figures that circulate in sim notes, were not opened from a manual. They stay out. Fin count is unpublished. The AIM-54B was not produced.

The C’s official prose says a digital processor, longer range, more altitude, better beam attack and ECM resistance, and a controlled-fragmentation warhead replacing the continuous rod. The spec line under that sentence still says **60 kg HE continuous rod**, fuze **active radar**, launch weight **463 kg**, and range **150 km**. The FAS text is more specific and does not say continuous rod: a DSU-28 target-detection device, the MK 82 Mod 0 on serials 83001–83054, and from serial 83055 the **WDU-29/B** with a **20–25 percent** increase in effectiveness. Parsch repeats that percentage and calls the WDU-29/B blast-fragmentation. The effectiveness gain is not a new maximum range. NAVAIR’s own chronology says both “1984 — AIM-54C reaches IOC” and “December 1986 — AIM-54C reaches Initial Operational Capability.” The training plan and Parsch say 1986 for the C and 1988 for ECCM/Sealed. Prefer **1986** and **1988**, and do not hide the 1984 line. ECCM/Sealed adds heaters and drops liquid cooling; the motor line is still Mk 47 Mod 1. A separate “High Power Phoenix” paragraph describes a travelling-wave-tube transmitter adapted from AMRAAM and a low-sidelobe antenna, with a direct hit on a QF-4 on 14 August 1990, and no separate weight or range. The Navy divested the missile on **30 September 2004**. The only family-level official quantitative range statement remains “in excess of 100 nautical miles.” The variant-block **135 km** and **150 km** stay on the page as contradictions, not as \(R_{\max}\). Sale of **274** missiles to Iran, approved in 1972 with final delivery in May 1979, is a chronology fact, not a second kinematic card ([NAVAIR Phoenix](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/12701); [FAS](https://web.archive.org/web/20160304112626/http://fas.org/man/dod-101/sys/missile/aim-54.htm)).

NAVAIR now marks AMRAAM range and speed **classified**. The USAF sheet of November 2007 says **20+ miles**, and its parenthesis **17.38+ nautical miles** is the conversion of 20 statute miles, not a claim of 20+ nm and not a kinematic maximum. That sheet’s airframe, not split by variant, is length **143.9 inches (366 cm)**, launch weight **335 pounds (150.75 kg)** — the pair does not match — diameter **7 inches**, wingspan **20.7 inches**, warhead “blast fragmentation” with no mass, power plant “high performance,” speed “supersonic” ([USAF fact sheet, November 2007](https://permanent.access.gpo.gov/lps53257/www.af.mil/information/factsheets/factsheet.asp-id=79.htm)). Its AIM-120D line is **358 pounds**. A Navy fact-file extract, the live page having returned Access Denied, puts A/B/C-4 at 348 pounds and C-5/6/7/D at **356 pounds**. Both official weights for the D are kept. Clipped span is the C/D family, tied in secondary writing to F-22 internal carriage. The A is not field-reprogrammable; the B, the C series, and the D are, on an ACC fact-sheet extract that also returned Access Denied when fetched directly ([NAVAIR AMRAAM](https://www.navair.navy.mil/product/AMRAAM); [Navy fact file, search-index extract](https://www.navy.mil/Resources/Fact-Files/Display-FactFiles/Article/2168352/aim-120-advanced-medium-range-air-to-air-missile-amraam/); [ACC fact sheet, search-index extract](https://www.acc.af.mil/Portals/92/Docs/Fact%20Sheets%20-%202020%20Update/Facts%20Sheets%202022%20Final/AIM-120_final.pdf)).

## Meteor throttles inlet air while MICA coasts

Meteor is not an AMRAAM with a larger range typed in. MBDA’s product page and 2023 datasheet give weight **190 kg**, length **3.7 m**, diameter **178 mm**, rail and ejection launch, an active RF seeker, inertial midcourse with a datalink, autonomous terminal guidance, an RF proximity fuze and impact, and a blast-fragmentation warhead. Saab’s length is **3.66 m** against MBDA’s 3.7 m, at the same 178 mm. ### A no-escape zone is the claim, not the radius

The kinematics that are publishable are the dependence, not a table. A 1948 NACA memo on a hypothetical gasoline and liquid-oxygen ducted rocket, not Meteor and not a solid fuel-rich gas generator, shows why one sea-level thrust-versus-time curve is the wrong object. With a fixed inlet, net thrust fell as altitude rose because captured airflow fell, and at fixed altitude thrust changed with Mach and with the air-to-fuel ratio. In that memo’s altitude case, net thrust at Mach 1 went from about **13,000 pounds** at sea level to about **8,000 pounds** at 45,000 feet. Those pounds belong to that unit. They are not pasted onto a **178 mm** missile, and neither is the memo’s frontal area ([NACA RM E7I05](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930085734/downloads/19930085734.pdf)). An abstract on throttleable ducted-rocket speed control, the PDF having been blocked, says a wide altitude band makes thrust variation mandatory, implemented as a variable throat on a pressure-sensitive solid burn rate, with a Mach controller outside that loop. It is not a Meteor valve schedule ([DTIC ADP005719, abstract](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/html/tr/ADP005719/index.html)). A Meteor card is therefore a booster impulse, unpublished, and then ramjet thrust as a function of altitude, Mach, and throttle or gas-generator flow, limited by remaining sustain propellant. Specific energy at intercept is an output. MBDA’s “several times” a current MRAAM, the RAF’s “very large” zone, and Saab’s “100+ km” are claims about that output. Survey figures of 200 km, Mach 4 exactly, or a 60 km no-escape zone were not on an opened primary page and are not stored. Cropped-fin and full-fin variants are on the MBDA drawing. Span is unpublished. Seeker band, waveform, acquisition range, and warhead mass are unpublished. Lock-on before launch is published for MICA and is not published for Meteor. Do not copy it across.

RF MICA is the other fielded European active-radar round, and it is a different motor. MBDA’s sheet gives **112 kg**, **3.1 m**, **160 mm** for the radar and infrared rounds together, a high-impulse low-smoke solid propellant, thrust-vector control, long-chord wings, tail surfaces, an **active RF monopulse Doppler** seeker, a strapdown inertial unit, a datalink, and both lock-on before launch and lock-on after launch. The sheet does not call the datalink two-way and does not give a band. Fusing is an RF proximity fuze, impact, and “focused splinters.” No thrust-time history, no burn time, and no warhead mass were on the opened pages; a 12 kg warhead figure was not confirmed. More than **5,900** MICA-family missiles sold, full-scale production, and service on Mirage 2000-5 and Rafale are MBDA statements. The infrared MICA is the same airframe with a passive dual-waveband imaging seeker. MBDA calls that seeker silent and gives it a 360° envelope including the rear sector. It is a Fox 2, not a second kinematic class ([MBDA MICA datasheet](https://www.mbda-systems.com/media/20541/download); [MBDA MICA](https://www.mbda-systems.com/products/air-dominance/mica-family/mica)).

MICA NG reprints **112 kg**, **3.1 m**, and **160 mm**, and changes the stores that matter: an **active RF AESA** seeker or a passive imaging infrared seeker, an explicit **two-way datalink**, lock-on before and after launch, and a **dual-pulse** motor. “Up to **+40%**” versus MICA is a **manufacturer claim**, not a kilometre and not a thrust curve. The AESA wording is a stated change from “monopulse Doppler,” not a stated frequency. DGA’s 19 June 2025 communiqué confirms a bi-pulse motor in an official sentence, records a first development firing of the **infrared** version only, and points at first deliveries by **2030**. The electromagnetic round was not the round fired that day. MICA NG is a development missile. It is not marked fielded from the 2023 brochure, and the second pulse is not lit at a guessed time. CAMM, **99 kg**, **3.2 m**, **166 mm**, is described by MBDA as a soft-vertical-launch land and naval weapon. No opened page shows a fielded fighter-launched CAMM Fox 3 ([MBDA MICA NG datasheet](https://www.mbda-systems.com/sites/mbda/files/2024-07/2023%20MICA%20NG%20datasheet.pdf); [DGA, 19 June 2025](https://www.defense.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/ministere-armees/Communiqu%C3%A9_La%20DGA%20proc%C3%A8de%20au%20premier%20tir%20de%20d%C3%A9veloppement%20d%E2%80%99un%20missile%20MICA%20NG%20depuis%20un%20avion%20Rafale.pdf); [MBDA CAMM](https://www.mbda-systems.com/products/force-protection/camm-family/camm)).

## The R-37 is not a heavy R-77

For every Russian round that is actually fielded or on an opened export card, the published sequence is inertial flight, then radio correction, then an active terminal phase. In jamming, that page says the seeker can passively home on a jammer co-located with the target. The R-33 is inertial and then semi-active, so it is a Fox 1, and its infobox ranges are not filed. The active R-27EA was not produced. Wikipedia says the active R-27A was cancelled in 1989 after prototypes, and Missilery’s launch counts for the K-27A do not match Wikipedia’s; neither page is a production record ([Wikipedia R-33](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-33_(missile)); [Wikipedia R-27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-27_(air-to-air_missile)); [Missilery R-27](https://en.missilery.info/missile/p27)).

The R-77 airframe is the lattice-tail missile. Missilery prints hinge moment not above **1.5 kgf·m**, effectiveness to about **40°** angle of attack, a turn rate up to **150°/s**, and a folded fit in a **300 mm** square. The turn rate is a published claim, not a measured autopilot limit, and there is no thrust vectoring. Both sentences are kept, and neither is a \(C_D\) ([Missilery R-77](https://missilery.info/missile/r77); [pvo.guns.ru](http://pvo.guns.ru/r77/); [Wikipedia, grid fin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_fin)). ## Honest cards stop at I-Derby ER, Gökdoğan, and Astra

Outside those families, an honest card means an opened manufacturer or government page states the seeker class and, if a range is stored, stores it with that page’s conditions and label. They do not share a data field ([Pulsed rocket motor, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsed_rocket_motor); [Dual-thrust rocket motor, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual-thrust_rocket_motor)).

PL-17, in the opened RUSI pages, was in test in 2020: about **6 m**, a multimode seeker described as active radar plus infrared, a **400 km class** secondary estimate, a high-altitude cruise, prototypes on the J-16, aimed at tankers and ISTAR. Those pages do not say dual-pulse. The War Zone’s dual-pulse label and Wikipedia’s 2024 service entry cite sources this pass did not open. PL-21 has no opened manufacturer, placard, or government page that documents an active seeker on a missile shown to exist under that name. It is not armed ([GlobalSecurity, PL-12](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/china/pl-12.htm); [PL-21, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PL-21)).

### AAM-4’s range cell says unpublished, and Fakour-90 has no settled seeker

AAM-4’s active terminal seeker and its inertial-plus-command midcourse are Japanese Wikipedia’s citation of a 2002 technical-history volume that was not opened, not an MoD PDF this research rendered. The infobox, partly cited to a 2006 yearbook that was also not opened, gives diameter **20.3 cm**, length **366.7 cm**, span **78.7 cm**, mass **220 kg**, a directional warhead of **31.3 kg**, a solid motor, and speed Mach **4–5**. The range cell is **非公開**, not published. An unfootnoted “around 100 km” is not used. An unfootnoted “dual-thrust” gloss is not an MoD statement and is not dual-pulse. A 2000–2007 ducted-rocket contract is described as a research vehicle, not a fielded motor, and it has no range. The only government page that fully opened says AAM-4B finished practical tests from October 2007 to March 2009 and says nothing about the antenna. The active-phased-array seeker and the **1.2×** standoff and **1.4×** autonomous-guidance ratios are Wikipedia citations of MoD PDFs that 404’d or sat behind a cookie wall. A web-index extract of a FY2009 post-evaluation defines standoff range as the distance at which the missile enters autonomous guidance and confirms it was longer than AAM-4, with no kilometres and no 1.2 or 1.4 factor. Those ratios are not convertible into kilometres without the baseline the ministry did not print. English-language figures of 85, 100, 120, or 170 km, and a Ka-band gallium-nitride seeker, are not on the Japanese page and stay out ([99式空対空誘導弾, Japanese Wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/99%E5%BC%8F%E7%A9%BA%E5%AF%BE%E7%A9%BA%E8%AA%98%E5%B0%8E%E5%BC%BE); [JASDF ADTW](https://www.mod.go.jp/asdf/adtw/adm/shiken/kakoshiken_missile2.html)).

Fakour-90 is not filed as a Fox 3. Iranian media, as relayed by a Janes item Wikipedia cites and this pass did not re-open, are reported as claiming a range of **150 km**, Mach 5, and guidance independent of the launch aircraft’s radar. Photograph notes show stencils reading “M112 ROCKET MOTOR” and “AIM-23B,” and a cable duct the analyst matches to the Hawk rather than to the AIM-54. The same analyst writes that it remains open whether the round kept the Hawk’s semi-active guidance. Wikipedia’s infobox prints active radar, a two-way datalink, **450 kg**, **4 m**, and **370 mm**, cited to a blog that was not opened. The seeker field stays unresolved. Setting it to active, or to semi-active, would settle a dispute the opened sources leave open ([Fakour-90, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fakour-90); [IranGeomil, 24 Jul 2018](https://irangeomil.blogspot.com/2018/07/analysis-fakour-air-to-air-missile.html)).

The catalog rule that falls out of all five families is the same rule. Store one row per variant. Store motor class, fin type, mass and diameter where a page printed them, and the conflicts rather than the average. Store guidance as inertial plus a datalink, except Phoenix, which keeps semi-active midcourse, and except the R-33, which is not in this class. Store a two-way flag only where the page that belongs to that round says so: AIM-120D on the notification extracts, Meteor as a manufacturer claim from 2025 and from Saab, MICA NG and I-Derby ER on their brochures. Do not mark RF MICA or Gökdoğan two-way. Store home-on-jam for the AIM-120, for Gökdoğan, and as the R-77’s co-located-jammer sentence. Do not store it for Phoenix. Store a Russian capture range only next to its cross section, as a check, never as \(R_{\mathrm{pitbull}}\). Leave thrust, burn time, total impulse, seeker band, gimbal, search-basket angle, datalink rate, and lethal radius null. The **18 m** default sphere is a software fallback, not a warhead, and a Fox 3 row does not inherit it or any Fox 2 proximity value. Warhead mass, where a page printed one, still does not imply that radius. Phoenix keeps its continuous-rod versus blast-fragmentation conflict instead of a picked mass. Zones, when they are swept at all, use the same integrator and the same miss-at-sign-change test, and they contain no stored kilometre. A temporary detection threshold is allowed only while it is labeled temporary, and it is not written back onto the card as a lock range.

## Conclusion

The no-escape zone is the energy the motor can still add after the target turns, and open literature never hands that energy over as a radius. A Meteor that is an AMRAAM with Saab’s “100+ km” typed in, or a PL-15E with Bronk’s “200 km class” typed in, will miss wrongly even if the terminal command is Zarchan’s, because the command consumes whatever speed the thrust history and the drag integral left, and it consumes the line of sight the datalink actually built. A boost-coast solid is done adding energy when the grain is done. A dual-pulse solid adds more only while the second charge, whose ignition time was not published, is actually burning. A ducted rocket can still be adding energy at intercept, and that thrust is a function of the air the inlet is capturing, which is why one sea-level table cannot stand in for the flight. Multiplying “several times,” “50 percent,” or “+40%” recreates the brochure with extra arithmetic.

The other change is that a blank is a result. Most of these rounds do not yet have a computable fly-out, because the impulse, the band, and the lethal radius were not in the pages that opened. Filling the blank from the neighbouring variant — MICA’s lock modes on Meteor, Derby’s 23 kg on I-Derby ER, the domestic PL-15’s AESA on the export round, a Wikipedia dual-pulse on an export card that says dual-mode — is the same error as a hard-coded range. The shot stays unanswered until a thrust model is supplied and labeled as not from a manual. That unanswered shot is the honest output. A kilometre printed anyway would only look like a missile.
