# Missile guidance, propulsion, and fuzing for a physical flyout

Scope is system dynamics: how an air-to-air missile produces acceleration, steers from seeker angles, and decides a burst. Infrared signature, chaff radar cross-section, and flare watts are out of scope. Named-missile performance already written up in `reports/Famous Fox 2 missiles.md` and `research_notes/Famous Fox 2 missiles/` is not repeated. No classified range tables were used. DTIC PDF fetches on 2026-09-29 returned a maintenance page, so miss-distance relations are taken from Zarchan directly rather than from the DTIC reports that quote him.

Fox 2, Fox 1, and Fox 3 are the three homing modes below: passive infrared, semi-active radar, and active radar. The guidance law and the airframe do not care which band produced the line-of-sight rate. The seeker and the support link do.

## Proportional navigation and its variants

### Takeaway

The homing command used in textbooks is \(n_c = N' V_c \dot\lambda\), with \(N'\) usually 3 to 5, \(V_c = -\dot R > 0\) when range is closing, and \(\dot\lambda\) the line-of-sight rate; the command is perpendicular to the line of sight and in the same rotational sense as \(\dot\lambda\). Augmented proportional navigation adds \(\tfrac{1}{2} N' n_T\) only when a constant target acceleration normal to the line of sight is known. Pure pursuit, lead or deviated pursuit, and command-to-line-of-sight are different laws and are not proportional navigation.

### Cited Findings

- Zarchan writes the proportional-navigation command as \(n_c = N' V_c \dot\lambda\), where \(n_c\) is the acceleration command, \(N'\) is a dimensionless designer-chosen gain “usually in the range of 3–5” called the effective navigation ratio, \(V_c\) is missile–target closing velocity, and \(\lambda\) is the line-of-sight angle. Closing velocity is “the negative rate of change of the distance from the missile to the target.” The command he analyzes is perpendicular to the line of sight. — [Zarchan, *Tactical and Strategic Missile Guidance* (2012), archive text](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- The same chapter defines zero-effort miss as the miss if the target holds its present course and the missile makes no further correction. For constant velocities, \(\mathrm{ZEM} = y + \dot y\, t_{go}\) in the linearized relative coordinate, \(\dot\lambda = \mathrm{ZEM}_{PLOS}/(R\, t_{go})\), and with \(R \approx V_c t_{go}\) the command is \(n_c = N'\,\mathrm{ZEM}_{PLOS}/t_{go}^2\). Zero line-of-sight rate means the pair is already on a collision triangle, so no further command is required. — [Zarchan (2012)](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- If the target has a constant acceleration \(n_T\), Zarchan augments the zero-effort miss by \(\tfrac{1}{2} n_T t_{go}^2\). Augmented proportional navigation is then \(n_{c,\mathrm{APN}} = N'\,\mathrm{ZEM}/t_{go}^2 = N' V_c \dot\lambda + N' n_T / 2\). The archive OCR is damaged around that line; the following optimal-control derivation on the same pages is clean: minimizing the integral of acceleration squared yields \(n_c = 3(y + \dot y\, t_{go} + \tfrac{1}{2} n_T t_{go}^2)/t_{go}^2\), “simply augmented proportional navigation with an effective navigation ratio of 3.” \(N' = 3\) because that cost was used; a different cost gives a different \(N'\). — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 2 and ch. 8](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- Zero-lag APN peak acceleration against a constant target maneuver is \(n_{c,\max}|_{\mathrm{APN}} = 0.5 N' n_T\). At \(N' = 3\) that is half the acceleration proportional navigation needs. At \(N' = 5\), Zarchan says APN’s peak is larger than proportional navigation’s peak. APN is therefore not uniformly “half the g.” The extra term is feedforward of target acceleration; it is not produced by the line-of-sight rate alone. With a 1 s flight-control lag, \(N' = 4\), and a 3 g step, Zarchan’s adjoint shows APN and proportional navigation are not ordered the same way in miss: APN misses less on short flights, proportional navigation less on long flights, if the loop stays linear and unsaturated. — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 8](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- An independent derivation that follows Zarchan gets the same split: \(a_M = N V_c \dot\theta = N\,\mathrm{ZEM}/t_{go}^2\) and, with target acceleration, \(a_M = N V_c \dot\theta + N a_T/2\). — [Benson, impact-time thesis excerpt quoting Zarchan](https://scispace.com/pdf/general-vector-explicit-impact-time-and-angle-control-32gpfm95o4.pdf)
- Sign, from a course text rather than from Zarchan’s OCR: commanded lateral acceleration is proportional to closing velocity and to line-of-sight rate, \(N\) is usually between 3 and 5, and the acceleration is applied so the missile velocity vector rotates in the same sense as the line of sight. A line of sight rotating counterclockwise means the target’s velocity component normal to the line of sight exceeds the missile’s; turning the missile the same way increases the missile’s normal component and drives the line-of-sight rate down. Positive closing speed is the usual case until closest approach. — [Ghose, NPTEL missile guidance, ch. 6](https://archive.nptel.ac.in/content/storage2/courses/101108056/module5/lecture9.pdf)
- Wikipedia’s names do not match Zarchan’s geometry. It calls pure proportional navigation \(a_n = N \dot\lambda V\) with \(a_n\) perpendicular to the missile velocity, \(N\) an integer generally 3–5, and \(V\) “the closing velocity.” It calls true proportional navigation the acceleration perpendicular to the line of sight, \(\vec a = -N|\vec V_r|\,(\vec R/|\vec R|)\times\vec\Omega\) with \(\vec\Omega = (\vec R\times\vec V_r)/(\vec R\cdot\vec R)\). A third, energy-conserving command is perpendicular to missile velocity because control surfaces cannot add speed. Generalized proportional navigation holds a constant angle between the acceleration and the line of sight. Augmented proportional navigation “adds a term to compensate for target acceleration (if it can be measured).” — [Wikipedia, Proportional navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_navigation)
- Early Sidewinder hardware did not form \(N' V_c \dot\lambda\) as a number. A gimballed spinning mirror was precessed onto the infrared source; the same voltage drove the control surfaces, so missile-velocity rotation was made proportional to line-of-sight rotation. That is not exactly proportional navigation at varying airspeed. — [Wikipedia, Proportional navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_navigation)
- Pure pursuit keeps the missile pointed at the present target. Attitude pursuit nulls the angle between the body axis and the line of sight; velocity pursuit nulls the angle between the velocity vector and the line of sight. Those differ by angle of attack. Deviated pursuit holds a fixed lead angle ahead of the line of sight instead of zero. Both pursuit and line-of-sight guidance demand high lateral acceleration near intercept even against a non-maneuvering inbound target, and that saturation causes miss. Proportional navigation’s terminal acceleration stays moderate against a non-maneuvering target because it starts removing line-of-sight rate early; it does not, by itself, account for target acceleration. — [Ghose, NPTEL, ch. 6](https://archive.nptel.ac.in/content/storage2/courses/101108056/module5/lecture9.pdf)
- Command-to-line-of-sight and beam riding are three-point laws. The reference line is the line from the control point to the target, not the missile-to-target line. The kinematic requirement is \(V_p = R\dot\theta\): the missile’s velocity perpendicular to that line equals the line’s tangential speed at the missile. Command-to-line-of-sight tracks missile and target at the launcher and uplinks acceleration. Beam riding points a beam at the target and the missile steers to the beam center. Neither is fire-and-forget. Terminal acceleration is high, and saturation again produces miss. — [Ghose, NPTEL, ch. 6](https://archive.nptel.ac.in/content/storage2/courses/101108056/module5/lecture9.pdf)

### Inferences

- A simulator has to pick one geometry and keep it. Zarchan’s scalar law is true-PN in the Wikipedia sense: accelerate perpendicular to the line of sight by \(N' V_c \dot\lambda\), same rotational sense as \(\dot\lambda\), with \(V_c = -\dot R\). The energy-conserving airframe command is that vector with the component along the missile velocity removed, or the pure-PN form that builds the command already perpendicular to velocity. Using \(V_m\) instead of \(V_c\) is a different law: it keeps authority when \(V_c\) is near zero or negative, and it is not the law Zarchan linearizes.
- \(N'\) between 3 and 5 is the practical band because \(N' = 3\) minimizes \(\int n_c^2\) for the zero-lag problem, higher \(N'\) front-loads the turn and settles the line of sight sooner, and values near 2 do not give the decaying acceleration histories Zarchan plots. This note does not treat “\(N' > 2\) or the loop is unstable” as a quoted theorem; that bound was not on the Zarchan pages opened here.
- APN’s extra term is used only when an estimate of target acceleration perpendicular to the line of sight exists (inertial midcourse, a datalink, or a differentiated line-of-sight model). A passive infrared seeker that outputs only angle and angle rate does not measure \(n_T\). Putting a constant \(N' n_T/2\) into an infrared homing loop without that estimate is not APN.
- Lead pursuit in the gunnery sense (point the velocity at the future collision point) is the constant-bearing triangle Zarchan already uses as the proportional-navigation equilibrium. Deviated pursuit, a fixed lead angle that is not the collision lead, is the NPTEL law and is not that triangle. Pure pursuit is lead angle zero and is what a seeker-boresight-equals-velocity loop flies if the navigation gain on line-of-sight rate is replaced by a gain on boresight error.

### Gaps

- Zarchan’s printed scalar \(n_c = N' V_c \dot\lambda\) and Wikipedia’s vector true-PN formula were not algebraically reconciled on an opened page. Use Zarchan’s scalar definition for the planar loop and treat the Wikipedia cross product as a 3-D statement of “perpendicular to the line of sight,” not as a proven identical sign.
- The closed-form zero-lag acceleration \(n_c = [N'/(N'-2)] n_T [1 - t/t_F]^{N'-2}\) was not recovered cleanly from the archive OCR, so it is not quoted. The APN peak \(0.5 N' n_T\) and the \(N' = 3\) versus \(N' = 5\) comparison were.
- Siouris and Garnell were not opened far enough to quote. NPTEL and Zarchan cover the same laws.

## Airframe lag, load factor, and miss distance

### Takeaway

Miss distance is the range at the instant closing velocity changes sign. A guidance lag \(1/(1+sT)\) makes a step target maneuver produce a miss of order \(n_T T^2\) when the maneuver’s time-to-go is a few time constants, and essentially zero miss when time-to-go is an order of magnitude larger than \(T\). Angle-of-attack and structural limits saturate acceleration and add still more miss; the dangerous endgame is \(t_{go}\) comparable to \(T\), not the mathematical limit \(t_{go}\to 0\).

### Cited Findings

- Zarchan defines miss distance as the missile–target separation at the point of closest approach, and he stops the engagement simulation when closing velocity changes sign because that is the minimum separation. — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 2](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- The homing loop he adjoints lumps seeker, noise filter, and flight control into one guidance-system time constant \(T\). For a single lag the guidance transfer used in the adjoint is \(W(s) = N' / [s(1+sT)]\). A binomial of order \(n\), \(1/(1 + sT/n)^n\), keeps that same effective \(T\) at every order. Two of five binomial lags are assigned to the seeker and the noise filter and three to the flight-control system in his saturation study. — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 3 and ch. 6](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- Adjoint construction: an impulse applied to the original system at time 0 and read at \(t_0\) is the same information as an impulse applied in the adjoint at \(t_F - t_0\) and read at \(t_F\). One adjoint run therefore supplies miss versus every flight time. The adjoint output Zarchan labels \(x_1\) is a miss sensitivity; multiply by the target acceleration \(n_T\) to get miss. Heading-error miss is that run’s other output times \(-\mathrm{HE}\). For flights that are long compared with \(T\), or for a maneuver that starts at the beginning of such a flight (large time-to-go), the miss is “virtually zero.” — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 3](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- For a single-lag loop at \(N' = 3\), the archive text gives the step-maneuver miss function \(\mathrm{MNT}|_{N'=3} = 0.5\, t_F^2 e^{-t_F/T}\), and it says adjoint time \(t_F\) “can be interpreted as either time of flight or time to go at which the maneuver occurs.” The same pages say the adjoint signal is multiplied by \(n_T\) to produce a length. Read together, the length is \(n_T\) times that expression. The OCR may have dropped a factor; the \(t_F^2 e^{-t_F/T}\) shape and the multiply-by-\(n_T\) rule are what the opened text actually supports. — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 3 and ch. 4](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- A single-lag model “seriously underestimate[s]” target-maneuver miss, relative to a higher-order binomial with the same effective \(T\), when flight time is not an order of magnitude greater than \(T\). The worked plot is a 3 g maneuver, \(N' = 4\), \(T = 1\,\mathrm{s}\). If several time constants are widely spaced, the slowest one dominates. Zero-lag and single-lag already disagree when flight time is not “significantly larger” than \(T\). — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 6](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- Short flights are not uniformly the worst case. For a target maneuver whose start time is uniform over the flight, Zarchan finds small miss at very short flight times “because the miss distance does not have enough time to develop,” and small miss again at long flight times because guidance nulls it. Separately, he writes that target maneuver induces “very large miss distances if the effective guidance time constant is too large or if the flight time is very short.” Both statements are in the opened text: geometric separation from a maneuver needs time to grow, and a guidance lag leaves a large residual when the remaining time is only a few \(T\). — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 4 and ch. 6](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- Saturation adds miss beyond the linear adjoint. “In endoatmospheric interceptors, angle-of-attack constraints limit maximum achievable accelerations at high altitudes, whereas the missile structure limits achievable acceleration levels at the lower altitudes.” He draws the limit on the commanded acceleration. Proportional navigation’s required acceleration against a maneuvering target grows toward intercept, so the designer has to have acceleration left at the end of flight or the missile saturates and misses. — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 2 and ch. 6](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)

### Inferences

- The phrase “miss grows when time-to-go is shorter than the airframe time constant” is the comparison against a long engagement, not a claim that miss increases all the way to \(t_{go} = 0\). On Zarchan’s \(0.5 t^2 e^{-t/T}\) shape, the sensitivity peaks near \(t_{go} \approx 2T\) and is small both for \(t_{go}\gg T\) and for \(t_{go}\to 0\). A real-time sim should integrate the lag; it should not add a canned miss penalty inside an arbitrary \(t_{go}<T\) gate.
- Load factor available from the airframe at a given state is the minimum of the structural limit and \(q S C_{N,\max}/m\). The second term is the angle-of-attack limit written as acceleration: high altitude and low dynamic pressure reduce it, which is Zarchan’s high-altitude case. Control-surface saturation is the actuator reason that limit is reached; Zarchan does not need a fin-deflection state to predict the miss, but a sim that already integrates angle of attack should clamp surface deflection and let normal force follow, rather than also clamping the guidance command to the same number a second time.
- First-order autopilot \(\dot n = (n_c - n)/T\), with \(T\) on the order of a few tenths of a second for a tactical missile, is the smallest model that reproduces the adjoint result. Instantaneous \(n = n_c\) is Zarchan’s zero-lag loop, which he says is optimistic whenever flight time is not much larger than \(T\).

### Gaps

- No opened page states a universal tactical-missile autopilot time constant. Zarchan’s numerical examples use \(T = 1\,\mathrm{s}\) because the plots are normalized, not because an air-to-air autopilot is that slow. The sim’s \(T\) has to be a data parameter.
- A separate aerodynamic incidence-lag formula \(T_\alpha = 2m/(\rho V S C_{N\alpha})\) was not on the pages opened here. It may be folded into Zarchan’s flight-control part of \(T\), but that identification is not sourced.
- The exact algebraic heading-error miss for \(N' = 4\) was OCR-broken and is omitted.

## Propulsion, drag, and specific energy

### Takeaway

Thrust is momentum flux plus a pressure term \((p_e - p_a)A_e\), so it rises as altitude increases for a fixed nozzle. Specific impulse links thrust to mass flow by \(I_{sp} = F/(\dot m g_0)\) only while both are constant. A boost–sustain or boost–coast motor whose chamber pressure is set by a changing grain needs a thrust-versus-time table; \(C_D\) needs a Mach table because wave drag is inside \(C_D\). Specific energy \(h + V^2/(2g)\) and specific excess power \(V(T-D)/W\) are what a kinematic envelope is measuring.

### Cited Findings

- Sutton’s time-averaged specific impulse is \(I_s = \int F\,dt / (g_0 \int \dot m\,dt)\), and \(I_s = I_t/(m_p g_0)\). For constant thrust, constant mass flow, and negligible start and shutdown transients this reduces to \(I_s = F/(\dot m g_0) = F/\dot w\). The preview states that specific impulse is thrust per unit propellant weight flow, and that the integral form is the one to use when thrust varies with time. — [Sutton, Biblarz, and Morehart, *Rocket Propulsion Elements*, 10th ed., preview of ch. 2](https://cdp.pageplace.de/cdp/public/publications/DT0400/9781394187225_A64502498/PREVIEW/9781394187225_A64502498_preview.pdf)
- The same preview’s worked nozzle example treats momentum thrust as invariant with altitude and computes a separate pressure thrust \((p_2 - p_3)A_2\), which is negative at sea level when the nozzle is over-expanded. Mass flow in that example is propellant mass expended divided by burn time. — [Sutton preview](https://cdp.pageplace.de/cdp/public/publications/DT0400/9781394187225_A64502498/PREVIEW/9781394187225_A64502498_preview.pdf)
- Cantwell: specific impulse is defined on total propellant mass flow for a rocket, and it increases as nozzle expansion ratio increases and as ambient pressure decreases. Ideal fully expanded solid-rocket specific impulse is typically 230–290 s. A meaningful \(I_{sp}\) states chamber pressure, area ratio, and whether the number is ideal or delivered. Thrust is the momentum flux of the exhaust plus the pressure contribution; for a large expansion ratio the pressure contribution becomes a smaller fraction of thrust. — [Cantwell, AA283 notes, ch. 7, 2024](http://web.stanford.edu/~cantwell/AA283_Course_Material/AA283_Lectures/AA283_Chapter_07_Rocket_Performance_Brian_J_Cantwell_2024.pdf)
- NASA’s drag equation is \(D = C_D \rho V^2 A / 2\). \(C_D\) folds in form drag, skin friction, wave drag, and induced drag, and it depends on shape, inclination, viscosity, and compressibility. Coefficients are experimental. The reference area must be the area used to reduce the data; body cross-section and wing area give different numbers for the same force. Dynamic pressure is \(\rho V^2/2\). — [NASA Glenn, Drag Equation](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/drag-equation/)
- Aircraft specific energy, as stated for aircraft and missile trajectory work, is the energy height \(V^2/(2g) + h\). The page marks the formula citation-needed, but it does state it, and it says lower density at altitude extends munition range through lower drag at a given speed. — [Wikipedia, Aircraft specific energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft_specific_energy)
- Specific excess power is \(P_s = V(T - D)/W\). The energy–maneuverability page calls \(P_s\) the vehicle’s net energy generation (or loss) and says it is directly proportional to potential climb rate. It applies in a turn, not only in a climb: positive \(P_s\) means a performance parameter can change without giving up another. — [Wikipedia, Energy–maneuverability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy%E2%80%93maneuverability_theory)
- A coasting missile is treated in the open literature as an energy-bleeding pursuer: once the motor is out, kinetic evasion works by exhausting the missile’s energy so that rendezvous never occurs. The dynamic-escape zone is computed from missile and aircraft parameters at the current line of sight, not from a stored range. — [Alkaher and Moshaiov, JGCD 2015, abstract](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.g000776)

### Inferences

- What must be a table is \(F(t)\) through the burn whenever the motor is boost–sustain, boost–coast, or any other grain that does not hold chamber pressure constant. Sutton’s constant-\(F\) specific-impulse formula is then only a per-segment converter: \(\dot m(t) = F_{\mathrm{mom}}(t)/(I_{sp} g_0)\), with propellant mass the integral of \(\dot m\). Altitude does not belong inside that table if the table is momentum thrust or vacuum thrust; it belongs in \((p_e - p_a(h))A_e\). If the only published number is total impulse and burn time, a flat thrust over that interval is an assumption, not a measured curve. An analytic curve is justified only for that rectangular idealization, or for a real \(r = a p_c^n\) grain model, which is more motor interior ballistics than a flight sim needs.
- Coast drag is \(D = q S C_D(M,\alpha)\). \(C_D(M)\) has to be tabulated or fitted because wave drag is part of \(C_D\), not a separate Mach “fudge” on top of a constant. Induced drag grows with the lift or normal force the guidance law is demanding, so a coast at high \(g\) bleeds energy faster than a zero-α coast. Density hits both drag and available load factor.
- Combining the two Wikipedia definitions gives \(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t\left(h + V^2/(2g)\right) = V(T-D)/W\) along the flight path, after the gravity component of \(\dot V\) cancels the kinematic climb term. The energy–maneuverability page says “proportional” rather than “equal”; the equality is the inference. A missile that still has specific energy can trade altitude for speed, but only if \(C_{N,\max}\) and the seeker still allow a turn. Energy alone is not a kill.

### Gaps

- The Sutton preview’s text layer did not yield a clean \(F = \dot m v_e + (p_e - p_a)A_e\) line. The pressure-thrust numerical example and Cantwell’s “momentum plus pressure” statement are the opened support. The Wikipedia rocket-engine page was fetched and carries a large-language-model warning, so it is not used.
- No opened source in this pass prints a generic boost–sustain thrust schedule or a tactical-missile \(I_{sp}\). Cantwell’s 230–290 s band is for ideal fully expanded solids in general, not an air-to-air motor.
- The aircraft-specific-energy formula is explicitly under-cited on the page that states it.

## Seeker geometry, shared by infrared and radar

### Takeaway

Infrared and radar homing seekers are the same dynamic object in the guidance loop: a gimballed boresight with a field of view, a field of regard, a rate limit, and a lag, outputting line-of-sight angle or rate. Lock-on-before-launch closes that loop at \(t = 0\) inside the gimbal. Lock-on-after-launch flies a cued or datalinked trajectory until range and look angle enter the capture set, then closes the same loop, usually with a heading error.

### Cited Findings

- A homing seeker “perceives the target, extracts tracking data from the received signal, and computes its own steering commands.” As range falls, a fixed angular error becomes a smaller linear error. That geometric advantage is independent of radio or infrared wavelength. Command and beam-riding errors do the opposite: a fixed angular error at the launcher becomes a larger linear error at longer range. — [Ivanov, in Skolnik, *Radar Handbook*, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)
- The Sidewinder implementation sensed directional error of a gimballed seeker, torqued the seeker onto the source, and used that torque command as the airframe command. The seeker output that enters guidance is an angle error, or the rate required to keep the error nulled, not a target identity. — [Wikipedia, Proportional navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_navigation)
- Instantaneous field of view is the cone the detector sees at a fixed seeker orientation. The overall field of view, also called tracking angle or off-boresight capability, includes gimbal travel. Because the assembly cannot move instantly, a target can leave the instantaneous field of view; the resulting limit is tracking rate, in angle per unit time. The page’s specific suggestions of “one degree” and “ten degrees” are marked citation-needed and are not used here as data. — [Wikipedia, Infrared homing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_homing)
- The same article describes a post-launch sequence in which the missile is fired at a target outside the seeker view, then “orient[s] itself in the direction indicated by the launcher and then attempt[s] to lock on.” That is lock-on-after-launch kinematics: an uplinked or sight-cued direction, then a search, then a track. — [Wikipedia, Infrared homing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_homing)
- Skolnik’s long-range pattern is the radar version of the same sequence. Active seekers are used all the way only at short range; otherwise a midcourse phase (inertial, beam rider, or command) carries the missile to the terminal region, “typically the last 10 guidance time constants or a few kilometers from intercept.” Pre-launch data or command updates initialize seeker angle and range or Doppler. Early continuous-wave semi-active missiles homed from launch to intercept; later designs home for about the last few seconds, again described as about ten guidance time constants. — [Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)
- NPTEL: on a homing missile the line-of-sight rate is the turn rate of the seeker that is tracking the target, and closing velocity comes from a Doppler measurement when the seeker is a radar. — [Ghose, NPTEL, ch. 6](https://archive.nptel.ac.in/content/storage2/courses/101108056/module5/lecture9.pdf)
- Zarchan puts seeker and noise-filter dynamics inside the guidance time constant, as two lags in the fifth-order example. A signal-processing delay is therefore not a separate miss source on top of \(T\); it is part of \(T\). — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 6](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)

### Inferences

- Engagement angles, all measured in the same inertial frame. Line of sight is the unit vector from missile to target. Lead angle is the angle between missile velocity and that line; it is zero in pure pursuit, and it is the collision lead when line-of-sight rate is zero. Aspect is the angle between the target nose (or target velocity) and the line from the target back to the missile. Look angle, or gimbal angle, is the angle between the missile body axis and the seeker boresight. Off-boresight at launch is the look angle at \(t = 0\) if the seeker is locked, or the cue angle if it is not. Field of view limits how far the target may sit from the boresight before the detector loses it. Field of regard is the set of look angles the gimbals can reach. A body-rate disturbance that the stabilization loop cannot cancel walks the boresight off the target even when the gimbal stops have not been hit.
- From the constant-bearing condition (zero line-of-sight rate) the perpendicular velocity components match: \(V_m \sin L = V_t \sin A\) in a planar triangle, with \(L\) the lead angle and \(A\) the target aspect from the line of sight. That identity is an inference from Zarchan’s and NPTEL’s collision statement, not a formula copied from a figure in those notes.
- Lock-on-before-launch: at release the seeker is inside its field of view and field of regard, so \(\lambda\) and \(\dot\lambda\) exist and the homing loop starts immediately. The launch envelope is the intersection of kinematics and that angular window. Lock-on-after-launch: until acquisition the guidance command is not a function of target line-of-sight rate. It is a function of the cued heading, an inertial aim point, or datalink states. When the seeker locks, proportional navigation sees a heading error equal to the angle between current velocity and the collision heading, and Zarchan’s heading-error miss applies to the remaining time-to-go. If that remaining time is only a few guidance time constants, the adjoint says the heading error is not driven out.

### Gaps

- No opened textbook page supplied a numeric gimbal-rate or field-of-view default that is both sourced and free of a citation-needed tag. Those limits are data, per missile, not constants of the guidance law.
- Radome aberration (boresight error proportional to gimbal angle) and glint were not opened in a citable form. They are seeker-error inputs to \(\dot\lambda\), not a change of guidance law.

## Radar homing: semi-active, active, and what “support” means

### Takeaway

Semi-active homing tracks target-reflected illumination and needs a rear reference so the seeker can form a Doppler frequency. Active homing is a small monostatic radar on the missile and is usually only the terminal phase; midcourse datalink or inertial flight has to deliver the seeker into an angle and range window. “Supported” and “unsupported” were not found as formal textbook terms; the physical split is whether that illumination, or those state updates, are still arriving.

### Cited Findings

- Skolnik’s categories: passive homing uses energy that originates at the target (jamming or the target’s own radar); active homing transmits from the missile and tracks the reflection; semi-active homing uses an external illuminator and a missile receiver. Continuous-wave semi-active homing was the most common operational waveform for many years. Active seekers cannot use continuous wave, because a single antenna cannot isolate the transmitter from the receiver; they use noncoherent pulse or coherent pulse-Doppler. The active reference is a sample of the missile’s own exciter. The semi-active reference is a rear receiver. — [Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)
- Semi-active geometry: the illuminator holds the target in its beam for the engagement. The missile takes the reflection on a forward antenna and a direct sample, often through illuminator sidelobes, on a rearward antenna. Mixing those two signals produces a Doppler tone “roughly proportional to closing velocity.” Rear Doppler depends on the missile’s radial velocity relative to the illuminator. Front Doppler depends on the radial velocities of illuminator, missile, and target. For a stationary illuminator and a head-on collision, \(f_d = (f_0/c)\, 2 V_c\). At X-band the rule of thumb given is 20 Hz of Doppler per foot per second of closing velocity. Feedthrough of the rear signal into the front receiver sits at an offset frequency; clutter occupies the spectrum between that and a return near twice missile speed. — [Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)
- The illuminator can be a continuous-wave tracker, a continuous-wave transmitter slaved to another tracker, or continuous-wave energy injected into a pulse-Doppler antenna. The reference must be spectrally pure enough that the echo falls in the receiver bandwidth. Loss of that coherence is a loss of the Doppler track, not a change of the proportional-navigation gain. — [Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)
- Active range “will be considerably less” than a comparable semi-active seeker using a high-power, large-antenna illuminator, because of missile antenna size and transmitter power. Hence active all-the-way is a short-range mode, and active terminal is the end of a multimode flight. Midcourse updates supply the angle and the range or velocity that initialize the seeker. — [Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf)
- A shipboard continuous-wave illuminator described by Johns Hopkins APL puts a narrow beam on the target and a wide reference beam that covers the missile throughout flight, specifically so the missile has a Doppler frequency reference. The transmitter and the receiver are linked and not colocated, which is the definition of semi-active used there. The same missile can home passively on a jammer. A later mode commands the missile in midcourse over a datalink and uses the illuminator in terminal semi-active homing. — [Witte and Mayer, Johns Hopkins APL Technical Digest, vol. 2, no. 4, 1981](https://secwww.jhuapl.edu/techdigest/content/techdigest/pdf/V02-N04/02-04-Witte.pdf)
- A Navy training text: an active homing missile illuminates the target with its own transmitter; a semi-active missile uses illumination from the launching aircraft, and the AIM-7F/M in particular “depends on RF energy radiated by the launching aircraft and reflected by the target.” — [Navy electricity and electronics training series, ch. 3](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/policy/navy/nrtc/14313_ch3.pdf)
- An electronic-defense text states the operational consequence without using the words supported or unsupported: semi-active homing requires the illuminator to stay on the target for the whole time of flight, and that is a disadvantage for the launching aircraft. Active seekers carry their own transmitter and, after acquisition, need no further assistance; the usual pattern is inertial or command midcourse, then onboard acquisition, then terminal active homing. — [Neri, *Introduction to Electronic Defense Systems*, 2nd ed., excerpt](https://vdoc.pub/documents/introduction-to-electronic-defense-systems-second-edition-artech-house-radar-library-6osefd4a77d0)
- Passive infrared homing is the third sensor on the same guidance law. The missile seeks “some physical indication from the target itself”; the training text names radar reflections and thermal characteristics as the two cases, and classifies homing as active, semi-active, or passive. — [Navy training series, ch. 3](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/library/policy/navy/nrtc/14313_ch3.pdf)

### Inferences

- Map the sim flags onto those conditions and do not invent a third guidance law. Semi-active (Fox 1) is supported while both are true: the illuminator beam still contains the target, and the missile still receives a rear reference coherent with that illumination. The seeker then outputs line-of-sight rate of the Doppler-resolved reflection, and proportional navigation runs. If either condition fails, the seeker output should drop out or coast, not silently track a perfect geometric line of sight. There is no range measurement in continuous-wave semi-active unless another sensor provides it; closing speed is the Doppler tone, which matches NPTEL’s statement that radar homing gets \(V_c\) from Doppler and \(\dot\lambda\) from the gimbal.
- Active terminal (Fox 3) is unsupported by the illuminator once the missile transmitter is the source. It is still dependent on midcourse support until acquisition: datalink or pre-launch states have to place the target inside the seeker field of view and inside the range and Doppler gate. After lock, guidance is ordinary proportional navigation on the onboard line-of-sight rate, and \(V_c\) can be measured. If updates stop early, the missile propagates the last target state. Zarchan’s zero-effort miss with constant velocities is exactly that propagation; a maneuvering target walks out of the basket by about \(\tfrac{1}{2} n_T t^2\). That is kinematics, not a tactic.
- Infrared (Fox 2) has no illuminator and no Doppler rear reference. Support, if any, is a cue that slews the seeker before or just after launch (lock-on-before-launch versus the cued lock-on-after-launch sequence above). Once locked, the loop is autonomous. A second infrared source is a seeker-output problem, covered in the countermeasure section, not a loss of illumination.
- The monostatic range equation was not printed on a page opened in this pass. Qualitatively, Skolnik’s comparison is enough for the sim: active acquisition range is a data parameter much shorter than the kinematic range, and semi-active acquisition depends on illuminator power and on two paths (illuminator to target, target to missile). Do not hard-code a detection range from an unsourced equation.

### Gaps

- The words “supported” and “unsupported” were not found as defined terms in the opened sources. The mapping above is an inference from illumination-maintained and datalink-initialized, which are the phrases the sources do use.
- James, *Radar Homing Guidance for Tactical Missiles*, turned up in search with a bistatic range equation and worked numerical examples. Those pages were not opened, and the numbers are not repeated, because a worked example is easy to misread as a weapon range.
- Home-on-jam is stated by Skolnik and by Witte as passive homing on the jammer. How the Doppler tracker decides to switch was not opened and is not specified here.

## Fuze, miss vector, and how to score a hit

### Takeaway

Score a hit from the miss vector at closest approach and from whether the target passed through the fuze’s detection volume, then apply a lethal-radius parameter. A proximity fuze is a range-gated or Doppler sensor with a shaped beam, and the preferred burst is generally not the geometric closest approach. A sphere around the target that detonates on first contact is a different model.

### Cited Findings

- Zarchan: miss distance is the separation at the sign change of closing velocity. The zero-effort miss is the predicted value of that separation if no further missile acceleration is applied. — [Zarchan (2012), ch. 2](https://archive.org/stream/TacticalAndStrategicMissileGuidance2012/Tactical%20and%20Strategic%20Missile%20Guidance%20(2012)_djvu.txt)
- A fuze keeps the warhead safe, arms it, detects the target, and initiates the warhead. Proximity fuzes, also called target-detecting devices, sense the target at a distance and fire when the target is predicted to lie in the warhead’s damage volume. Modes include active, semi-active, and passive, using a reflected radio or radar signal, or an infrared signal, among other influences. — [Naval Postgraduate School fundamentals text, ch. 14, via archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20150504004413/http://fas.org/man/dod-101/navy/docs/fun/part14.htm)
- An active electromagnetic fuze is “a miniature radar set”: transmit, receive, and identify the return. A range gate fires when the round-trip delay falls to a set value. The maximum gate is a range cutoff, used so rain and the sea surface do not fill the receiver. A Doppler fuze classifies the return by radial speed. Head-on, Doppler is high and the warhead must be fired immediately; from the rear, Doppler is low and a delay may be required. Detonation “could occur when the Doppler shifts from an up to a down Doppler or at the point of closest approach.” — [NPS fuzing chapter](https://web.archive.org/web/20150504004413/http://fas.org/man/dod-101/navy/docs/fun/part14.htm)
- For an air target the preferred burst is not, in general, closest approach. The fuze tries to fire inside a lethal burst interval along the trajectory, so that fragments meet the vulnerable region. The text gives a delay \(T = M - N/V_c\), with \(V_c\) in meters per second and \(T\) in milliseconds, \(M\) and \(N\) constants or functions of the fuze. Closing speeds cited as the design range of that delay run from 180 to 1800 m/s or more. Fragments are concentrated in a narrow zone in the missile pitch plane rather than thrown isotropically, so the fuze’s job is to converge that beam with the target. The first detection of the target (point A) starts a timed flight to the burst point (point B). — [NPS fuzing chapter](https://web.archive.org/web/20150504004413/http://fas.org/man/dod-101/navy/docs/fun/part14.htm)
- The same chapter is explicit that a tactical warhead does not devastate a target “if detonated anywhere in its vicinity,” and that warhead size is limited by what the airframe can carry. Lethal radius in a simulator is therefore a data parameter standing in for that damage volume, not a consequence of the guidance law. The text does not publish a numerical lethal radius, and none is invented here.
- Safing and arming isolates the detonator until the weapon has launched and moved a safe distance. Missile devices commonly integrate rocket acceleration and require it to persist (the text’s examples are on the order of a second to ten seconds), so a short shock does not arm the round. A self-destruct or time-out safe is a separate timer for a miss that would otherwise fall back armed. — [NPS fuzing chapter](https://web.archive.org/web/20150504004413/http://fas.org/man/dod-101/navy/docs/fun/part14.htm)

### Inferences

- A physically consistent score, using only quantities the sources define, is:
  1. Record the miss vector at the sign change of \(V_c\), in the plane normal to the relative-velocity vector. That is Zarchan’s miss.
  2. Arm the fuze only after the sustained-acceleration or range gate the data card specifies. Before that, a small range is not a burst.
  3. Declare a fuze detection when the target enters a data-defined volume: a conical or fan beam at a fixed angle off the nose, intersected with a range gate (radar pulse delay or a laser time of flight) or, for a Doppler fuze, when the Doppler passes through zero near closest approach.
  4. Apply the delay \(T(V_c)\) and burst there. Score a kill if the predicted fragment travel meets the target, or, in the simplified parameter the sources allow without warhead design, if the miss-vector magnitude is inside the lethal radius and the fuze did detect. A fly-through of a sphere that never crosses the beam is a miss. A beam detection whose miss is outside the lethal radius is a fuze fire and a failed kill.
- Laser and radar fuzes are the same logic with a different sensor. The NPS text already lists infrared as a proximity influence and radar as the range-gated case. The beam shape is data. The guidance loop is unchanged.
- Impact fuzing is the limit of a range gate at zero, plus an optional delay. It should remain a separate branch so a proximity failure can still kill on contact.

### Gaps

- \(M\) and \(N\) in \(T = M - N/V_c\) are not given numerically. They are fuze-design parameters.
- No opened page gives a generic air-to-air lethal radius. Using one number as data is consistent with the sources; copying a number from a manual into this note is not, and was not done.
- Fragment beam angles and warhead layout were deliberately not researched.

## Countermeasures at the guidance-loop level

### Takeaway

A single-target tracker outputs one angle: the centroid of the sources inside its field of view, or whichever source remains after the other leaves that field. Proportional navigation then flies that false line of sight. No chaff or flare radiation model is required to state the loop effect.

### Cited Findings

- A second-course electronic-warfare text: an infrared tracker “tracks the centroid of all of the IR energy within its field of view.” A flare with more energy than the aircraft pulls that centroid toward the flare. As the flare separates, the centroid moves with it. When the aircraft leaves the tracking field of view, the missile homes on the flare alone. A two-color seeker can reject a flare that is hotter than the aircraft in the wrong band; fooling it requires the expendable to match the measured energy ratio, which is a seeker-discrimination fact, not a guidance-law fact. — [Adamy, *EW 102*, excerpt](https://vdoc.pub/documents/ew-102-a-second-course-in-electronic-warfare-1sjt1858j480)
- A Navy technical paper’s public description of a spin-scan reticle: before the decoy, the servo holds the target at the center of the field of view; after release, the servo tracks the centroid of the two images; when the decoy has separated enough that the aircraft image crosses the peak of the static-gain curve, the decoy remains in the track well and “the decoy has won.” — [NAWCWD TP 8773, as reproduced in the public infrared-countermeasures extract](https://metabunk.org/attachments/aircraft-infrared-principles-signatures-threats-and-countermeasuresada566304-pdf.61551/)
- A 2023 guidance paper treats an unresolved aircraft-plus-decoy as one virtual target at the energy centroid, \(N\) dividing the segment in proportion to the two infrared energies. The paper’s own separation rule, which is that paper’s model and not a standard seeker specification, is that the decoy is resolved when the angle between the two sources exceeds 60° or the decoy energy falls below 0.6 of the target. Until then, the guidance law is given the virtual target’s line-of-sight rate. — [Duan et al., Chinese Journal of Aeronautics, 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1000936123001851)
- Semi-active and active seekers can be pulled the same way by a second Doppler-resolved angle, and both Skolnik and Witte note a passive home-on-jam mode when the target radiates. The opened pages do not give the angle-tracker equation for two radio sources. — [Skolnik, ch. 19](https://ww.helitavia.com/skolnik/Skolnik_chapter_19.pdf); [Witte and Mayer, 1981](https://secwww.jhuapl.edu/techdigest/content/techdigest/pdf/V02-N04/02-04-Witte.pdf)

### Inferences

- The guidance law should subscribe only to the seeker boresight and its rate. While two sources are unresolved, that boresight is the centroid, so \(n_c = N' V_c \dot\lambda_{\mathrm{centroid}}\) steers between them, closer to the brighter source. When the angular separation exceeds the seeker’s resolution (instantaneous field of view, or an imaging gate), the output steps to one source. That step is a heading-error impulse into Zarchan’s loop. If the remaining time-to-go is a few guidance time constants, the missile cannot abandon the wrong source even if the seeker later reassigns the track.
- A radar seeker with two angle sources in one resolution cell does the same thing if its angle output is a centroid. Chaff that only changes a radar cross-section, without moving the angle the tracker reports, does not belong in this loop. Flare watts belong in the sensor note that decides which source is brighter and when the pair is resolved.

### Gaps

- The 60° and 0.6 energy-ratio figures are one paper’s simulation thresholds, not a measured seeker. Do not promote them to defaults.
- No opened source gave the analogous two-target monopulse equation. The inference that an unresolved pair becomes one line of sight is the infrared result applied to a tracker that has a single angle output.

## Kinematic envelope: no-escape zone and dynamic launch zone

### Takeaway

Both zones are sets of states, not stored ranges. The dynamic launch zone is the set of launch states from which integrating this missile model reaches a fuze-valid intercept. The no-escape zone is the subset that still does so when the target is allowed a defined set of maneuvers and aspects. Recompute them with the same integrator that flies the round.

### Cited Findings

- A 1996 AIAA flight-simulation paper constructs a shooter-centered no-escape zone as the union of launch-acceptability regions over target headings and maneuvers. “Anything within the region, regardless of heading or maneuver, is kinematically within a weapon’s capability.” It notes that an older dogfight missile can have an empty zone because its seeker restricts it to the rear hemisphere. The launch-acceptability region itself is the target-centered or shooter-centered set of positions for one assumed target motion. — [AIAA Flight Simulation Technologies Conference, 1996, archive text](https://archive.org/details/AIAA_Flight_Simulation_Technologies_Conference_Technical_Papers_July_29-31_1996)
- A 2008 paper defines the dynamic-launch-zone computation as numerical integration of the nonlinear missile and target equations, including a six-degree-of-freedom model and a guidance law, using measured aerodynamic and thrust data. Altitude changes the zone. The authors treat the zone as an output of the integration, not as an input table. — [Cernat and Chelaru, Argos Press, 2008, abstract](https://www.argospress.com/articles/2008/a-complex-mathematical-model-for-computation-of-the-dynamic-launch-zone-for-air-to-air-homing-missiles)
- A design-evaluation paper computes minimum and maximum launch ranges by repeated three-degree-of-freedom engagements, and stops a run when a seeker, structural, or terminal-energy criterion is violated. Those three are the gates on “the missile still has energy and seeker authority at intercept.” — [SciTePress, 2017](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2017/64709/64709.pdf)
- Alkaher and Moshaiov’s dynamic-escape zone is the in-flight counterpart for a coasting missile: the set of states along the current line of sight from which the aircraft can still exhaust the missile’s energy and avoid rendezvous. It is recalculated from the current energy state. — [Alkaher and Moshaiov, 2015](https://doi.org/10.2514/1.g000776)
- A 2015 Chinese study distinguishes the launch-time envelope from the envelope of a missile that is already flying, and reports that the in-flight zone is not identical to the no-escape zone drawn at launch. Wind changes it. The useful point for a sim is that a canned launch chart is the envelope at one time. — [ResearchGate record of the 2015 dynamic-attack-zone paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282476438_Dynamic_attack_zone_of_air-to-air_missile_after_being_launched_in_random_wind_field)

### Inferences

- Compute the dynamic launch zone by integrating the same point-mass or six-degree-of-freedom model used in combat, from a grid of launch ranges, lead angles, altitudes, and speeds. A state is inside the zone if the run reaches Zarchan’s closest approach with the fuze armed, the miss inside the lethal-radius parameter, the seeker still inside gimbal and track-rate limits (or having locked, for lock-on-after-launch), and speed or specific energy above a declared floor. The inner boundary is the set of states that violate the arming gate or the minimum-range fuze cutoff. The outer boundary is energy or seeker loss.
- The no-escape zone is not a tighter canned range. It is the intersection, over a declared maneuver set and aspect set, of those dynamic launch zones. Change the maneuver set and the zone changes. Rear-aspect-only seekers can make the intersection empty, which is the 1996 paper’s observation, without any change to propulsion.
- Specific excess power enters because a state with \(T \le D\) and little speed cannot meet the terminal-energy gate after a long pursuit. That is why the envelope has to be integrated: \(P_s\) along a straight line does not know about the lead turn or the induced drag of a high-\(g\) correction.

### Gaps

- The Argos and ResearchGate items were opened only as abstracts or records, not as full papers. They support the method (integrate the model; do not store a range) and not a numerical zone.
- No opened source defines one standard target maneuver that makes a no-escape zone unique. The maneuver set is part of the definition and should be a sim input.

## Implementation notes for missilesim

### Takeaway

Replace the instantaneous lateral force and the sphere test with one state vector, a thrust and \(C_D\) table, a lagged acceleration command, a seeker that can lose lock, and a miss scored at closest approach against a fuze beam and a lethal radius. The current Fox 2 loop already has proportional navigation, a load-factor cap, and a gimbal cone; it does not have the lag, the line-of-sight-normal \(V_c\) law, or a fuze volume.

### Cited Findings

- `MissileFox2.cpp` forms \(\vec\Omega = (\vec R \times \vec V_{\mathrm{rel}})/R^2\) and commands \(N V_m (\vec\Omega \times \hat v)\) with \(N\) clamped to 3–5. The comment states the reason for \(V_m\): a closing-speed law loses authority when closing speed is small or negative. While heading error to a constant-velocity collision point is large, the command blends in a full-authority turn on top of proportional navigation. With no track, the command is a pursuit acceleration using a fixed lead time `kInertialLeadSeconds`. Achieved acceleration is `applyForce(commanded * mass)` in the same tick, limited by \(q S C_{N}/m\), structural \(g\), and thrust-vector acceleration while the motor burns. There is no autopilot state. — `C:\Users\alede\Documents\code\missilesim\src\objects\MissileFox2.cpp`
- `Fox2Flight.h` already documents a Mach- and dynamic-pressure-dependent body \(C_{D0}\), an altitude back-pressure on thrust, induced drag from an assumed fin aspect ratio, a seeker intensity that depends on aspect, and an unpublished track-rate stand-in of 180°/s so that the gimbal, not the rate, is what stops an unsourced seeker. Imaging and kinematic separation angles for a second source are constants in that header. Thrust for several rounds is an average impulse spread over a burn time because no thrust curve was published. — `C:\Users\alede\Documents\code\missilesim\src\sim\Fox2Flight.h`
- `PhysicsEngine::checkMissileTargetHit` arms on `isFuzeArmed()`, then detonates when the segment from the previous position to the current position intersects a sphere of target radius plus `getProximityFuseRadius()`. The user-interface copy for that radius is “Detonation distance from the target,” default 18 m. — `C:\Users\alede\Documents\code\missilesim\src\physics\PhysicsEngine.cpp`, `C:\Users\alede\Documents\code\missilesim\src\application\ApplicationUI.cpp`

### Inferences

State to integrate, one sample per tick:

- Kinematics: position, velocity, mass, and specific energy \(h + V^2/(2g)\) as a diagnostic, not as a second integrator.
- Propulsion: burn time, propellant mass, and thrust direction. Thrust is a table \(F_{\mathrm{mom}}(t)\) plus \((p_e - p_a)A_e\). Mass flow is \(F_{\mathrm{mom}}/(I_{sp} g_0)\) inside each constant-\(I_{sp}\) segment. A rectangular average is only the fallback already used when no curve exists.
- Aero: Mach, dynamic pressure, \(\alpha\), \(C_D(M,\alpha)\) or \(C_{D0}(M)\) plus induced drag, \(C_{N,\max}\). Drag opposes velocity. Available load factor is \(\min(n_{\mathrm{struct}}, q S C_{N,\max}/m)\) plus thrust-vector capability while burning.
- Seeker, shared by infrared and radar: boresight direction, gimbal angles, gimbal-rate state, lock flag, and a filter state for the processing lag. Data: instantaneous field of view, gimbal limit (field of regard), track-rate limit, and lag time. Lock-on-before-launch starts with lock set if the target is inside the gimbal. Lock-on-after-launch starts with lock clear and a cued boresight; lock sets when range, look angle, and, for radar, Doppler gate say so.
- Radar support flags, not extra guidance laws: illuminator on target, rear reference valid, datalink age, and active-seeker on. Semi-active line-of-sight rate is valid only while the first two are true. Active line-of-sight rate is valid only after the seeker has locked. Until then the command uses the last datalink zero-effort miss, propagated at constant target velocity.
- Guidance memory: filtered \(\dot\lambda\), estimated \(V_c = -\dot R\) (Doppler if the seeker is a radar; geometric only as a truth sensor), and optional estimated \(n_T\) for augmented proportional navigation.
- Fuze: armed flag, beam angles, range-gate or Doppler-zero logic, delay coefficients \(M\) and \(N\), lethal radius, and the miss vector captured when \(V_c\) changes sign.

Command each tick, after the seeker update:

1. If the seeker is locked, \(\vec\Omega\) from the lagged boresight rate, not from truth, except in a truth-sensor debug mode.
2. Default command matching Zarchan: \(n_c = N' V_c \dot\lambda\) perpendicular to the line of sight, same sense as \(\dot\lambda\), \(N'\) in 3–5. Project out the velocity component if the airframe cannot thrust along the body. The current \(N V_m (\vec\Omega \times \hat v)\) law can stay as an explicit option; it is not the same command, and the code comment already says why it was chosen.
3. Add \(N' n_{T\perp}/2\) only when a target-acceleration estimate exists.
4. Pure pursuit, if selected, nulls lead angle instead of \(\dot\lambda\). Command-to-line-of-sight does not belong on a fire-and-forget air-to-air round.
5. Pass \(n_c\) through \(\dot n = (n_c - n)/T\) and then the load-factor limit. Do not `applyForce` the raw command.
6. If a second source is unresolved, point the boresight at the centroid the sensor note provides, and let step 2 run unchanged. When the sources separate, step the boresight once.

What this replaces:

- The instantaneous force and the heading-error blend in `MissileFox2.cpp`. The blend is a heuristic around the missing autopilot and the missing collision-triangle transient. Zarchan’s heading-error response plus a time constant does that job without a second law.
- The inertial fixed-lead fallback, unless the seeker has actually dropped lock and the round is in a declared coast-on-memory mode. Memory should propagate the last target velocity for a short data-defined time, then go ballistic, not fly pure pursuit indefinitely.
- The sphere test in `PhysicsEngine::checkMissileTargetHit`. Keep the arming gate. Replace the radius check with beam-plus-miss-vector-plus-lethal-radius. The 18 m slider is a lethal radius or a range gate, not both; the interface should say which.
- A single average thrust, wherever a real \(F(t)\) table exists. Where the manual only gives impulse and burn time, keep the rectangular assumption and do not invent a boost–sustain shape.
- Any future no-escape or dynamic-launch display that stores a range. Sweep the same integrator.

### Gaps

- Seeker field of view, gimbal rate, autopilot \(T\), fuze beam angles, and lethal radius are not in the opened textbooks as values for this sim. They are data fields. Track rate is already per-round in the Fox 2 catalog when a source exists.
- This note did not re-derive the Fleeman \(C_D\) fit. `Fox2Flight.h` already points at `research_notes/Famous Fox 2 missiles/flight-model.md` for that.
- Active and semi-active missiles are not a separate flight class in the files read. The same state vector covers them; only the seeker’s validity conditions change.
