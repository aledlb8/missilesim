# Terrain, Earth Curvature, and Propagation Limits on Radar Visibility

Scope is geometry and propagation only: whether energy reaches the target and what the surface and the weather add as loss or as competing cross-section. Radar modes, waveforms beyond the resolution length that sets clutter area, and missile guidance are out of scope. The detection equation is assumed to consume three interfaces from this note: a two-way propagation factor (diffraction and multipath), a two-way atmospheric loss in dB, and a clutter cross-section σ_c (surface or weather) in m². No full radar-equation derivation is repeated.

Numbers are tagged as **standard** (ITU or the usual textbook convention), **measured** (fit to measurements), **model** (published empirical formula), or **rule of thumb**. Worked arithmetic is labeled as calculated from those formulas, not as a measurement. Wikipedia’s radar-horizon article carried an original-research warning when opened (July 2026); geometric formulas that match other sources are used, and unsourced operational claims on that page (clutter-zone width, MTI decibels) are not.

## Geometric line of sight over a spherical earth

### Takeaway

Replace an infinite flat plane with a sphere of effective radius a_e = k R_e. Under a standard atmosphere k ≈ 4/3, so each end of the path contributes a horizon distance d ≈ sqrt(2 a_e h) and the smooth-earth radio horizon between a radar and a target is the sum of the two. Antenna height and target altitude enter only as those two heights above the same datum; the 4/3 factor is a linear refractivity gradient, not a property of the radar.

### Cited Findings

- Without refraction, the distance from a radar at height H above a smooth sphere of radius R_e to the tangent point is D_h = sqrt(2 H R_e + H²). When H ≪ R_e this is D_h ≈ sqrt(2 H R_e). The fractional error of the approximation grows with height and is stated as less than 1% for H < 250 km. With a standard atmosphere the same article replaces R_e by (4/3) R_e in the approximate form, D_h = sqrt(2 H (4 R_e / 3)), and quotes an effective radius of about 8.5×10³ km against a geometric radius of about 6.4×10³ km. — [Wikipedia, Radar horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_horizon)
- The same page’s numerical examples, geometric radius only: a radar at 1.6 km has a horizon of 143 km, and one at 23 m over the ocean has a horizon of 16 km. With the 4/3 radius those become 164 km and 19 km. — [Wikipedia, Radar horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_horizon)
- A minimum target height for visibility at range R_T from a radar at height H is given there as H_T > (R_T − sqrt(2 H R_e))² / (2 R_e). As printed, this shadow formula uses the geometric radius even though the horizon formula immediately above it uses 4/3. Treat that mismatch as an inconsistency on the page, not as two different physical models. — [Wikipedia, Radar horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_horizon)
- The effective-earth-radius factor is k = R_e,eff / r, with r the physical radius. k = 1 is no refraction. k → ∞ is a flat earth. The usual choice is k = 4/3. That value corresponds to an index-of-refraction gradient of −39×10⁻⁹ m⁻¹. The gradient form is k = 1 / (1 + r · refgrad), with r in metres and refgrad = dn/dh. Default inputs in that implementation return R_e,eff = 8.4774×10⁶ m and k = 1.3306, which is the −39×10⁻⁹ gradient on a mean-radius earth, not the rounded fraction 4/3. — [MathWorks, effearthradius](https://www.mathworks.com/help/releases/R2024a/radar/ref/effearthradius.html)
- Refractivity N in N-units is defined by n = 1 + 10⁻⁶ N, so a gradient of −39 N-units per kilometre is the same number as dn/dh = −39×10⁻⁹ m⁻¹. The effective-radius method does not represent ducting. At long range and shallow angles, k can depart strongly from 4/3. An average-radius-of-curvature option in the same function depends on radar altitude, target altitude, and slant range; with default surface refractivity, a sea-level radar and an 8 km target at 100 km give R_e,eff = 7.4342×10⁶ m, and a 9 km target at 200 km gives 7.3525×10⁶ m, both below the 4/3 value. — [MathWorks, effearthradius](https://www.mathworks.com/help/releases/R2024a/radar/ref/effearthradius.html)
- The two-terminal horizon used for a surface radar and a raised target is R_horizon = sqrt(2 R_e k_e h_radar) + sqrt(2 R_e k_e h_target), with R_e = 6371 km and k_e = 4/3. Their worked case, h_radar = 30 m and h_target = 100 m, is 22.6 km + 41.2 km = 63.8 km. A 5 m sea-skimmer against the same 30 m antenna is 22.6 km + 9.2 km = 31.8 km. They state that standard refraction extends the horizon by about 15% over k_e = 1, that k_e > 2 can occur with ducting, and that k_e < 1 (subrefraction) shortens the horizon. Those k bounds are described, not tabulated from a sounding. — [RF Essentials, radar horizon](https://rfessentials.com/rf-knowledge-base/how-do-i-calculate-the-radar-horizon-for-a-surface-based-radar-detecting-low-alt/)
- An equivalent statement of the single-end distance is the exact tangent length d = sqrt(2 R_eff h_eff + h_eff²) with R_eff = k R, and the near-surface approximation d ≈ sqrt(2 R_eff h_eff). — [Lumen Calculator, radar horizon](https://lumencalculator.com/radar-horizon-calculator/)
- GDAL’s viewshed uses a different symbol for the same physics. Height is corrected as Height_corrected = Height_DEM − CurvCoeff · TargetDistance² / SphereDiameter. CurvCoeff = 1 − RefractionCoeff. No refraction is CurvCoeff = 1. Visible light uses refraction 1/7, so CurvCoeff ≈ 0.85714. Radio is listed as refraction coefficient 0.25 to 0.325, curvature coefficient 0.75 to 0.675, and sphere-diameter factor 1.33 to 1.48. Flat earth is CurvCoeff = 0. — [GDAL gdal_viewshed](https://gdal.org/en/stable/programs/gdal_viewshed.html)

### Inferences

- Units that stay consistent: R_e = 6.371×10⁶ m, h in metres, d in metres. a_e = k R_e. Exact horizon from one height: d = sqrt(2 a_e h + h²). Sum for a smooth sphere: D = d(h_radar) + d(h_target). Both heights are above the surface that defines the sphere (mean sea level if the earth is smooth water; DEM elevation plus antenna mast if the radar is on land). Using altitude above the ellipsoid for one end and orthometric height for the other shifts a low target by the geoid undulation.
- k = 4/3 exactly gives a_e = (4/3)×6.371×10⁶ = 8.495×10⁶ m. The −39×10⁻⁹ m⁻¹ gradient gives k = 1.3306 and a_e = 8.477×10⁶ m. The 0.2% difference is negligible next to real variations of k. Implement the gradient form if a sounding is ever supplied, and the constant 4/3 otherwise.
- Unit trap: dn/dh = −39×10⁻⁹ m⁻¹, while dN/dh = −39 N-units/km. Both are the standard atmosphere. Inserting metres into a formula written for kilometres, or N-units into a formula written for n, is a factor-of-10³ or 10⁶ error.
- GDAL’s radio curvature coefficient 0.75 is 1/k for k = 4/3, because drop below the local tangent plane is d² / (2 a_e) = d² / (k · diameter). A blog that calls a small decimal “k” is not using the radio k-factor.
- Converting the approximate 4/3 formula to the old manual form gives horizon distance in nautical miles ≈ 1.23 sqrt(height in feet), calculated from d = sqrt(2 k R_e h) with R_e = 6371 km. That is a unit conversion of the standard formula, not a separate measurement.
- The Wikipedia shadow equation should be evaluated with a_e, not with the unrefracted radius, if the rest of the sim uses 4/3. Equivalently, declare the target below the smooth-earth horizon when slant range exceeds d(h_radar) + d(h_target).

## Terrain masking

### Takeaway

On a real surface the smooth-earth horizon is only the upper bound. Sample a digital elevation model along the ground track, lift each sample by the earth bulge d1 d2 / (2 a_e), and compare that height with the straight ray drawn in the effective-earth frame. A local tangent plane plus that parabolic bulge is the model ITU-R P.526 and GDAL both use; a full WGS84 ray is not required for theater-scale ranges. The expensive product is the elevation raster, not the test.

### Cited Findings

- ITU-R P.526-16 builds an effective terrain height at profile point i as (h_t)_i = h_i + d_ui d_iv / (2 a_e), where h_i is the terrain height, d_ui and d_iv are distances from the point to the two ends of the path, and a_e is the effective earth radius. The ray height above the same sea-level chord is the linear interpolation (h_r)_i = (h_u d_iv + h_v d_ui) / d_uv. Clearance is ray minus terrain. The first-Fresnel radius at that point is F1 = sqrt(λ d_ui d_iv / d_uv), in self-consistent units. Normalized clearance C_F = h_z / F1, and the knife-edge parameter of the worst point is ν = −C_F sqrt(2). — [ITU-R P.526-16 (2025) PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.526-16-202511-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- GDAL’s viewshed implements the Wang, Robinson, and White (2000) reference-plane method, not a naive sightline per cell. It requires a projected coordinate system; results are not meaningful in raw geographic degrees. Observer height above the DEM defaults to 2, target height above the DEM defaults to 0, and both are in the DEM’s height unit. A maximum distance clamps the raster. Output can be a binary mask or the minimum target height that would be visible. Cumulative mode stores how many observers see each cell. Input nodata is not treated specially and can produce wrong visibility. — [GDAL gdal_viewshed](https://gdal.org/en/stable/programs/gdal_viewshed.html); method reference [Wang 2000](https://www.asprs.org/wp-content/uploads/pers/2000journal/january/2000_jan_87-90.pdf)
- A horizon algorithm used for sky-view factor casts rays in azimuth sectors (default 360) against a triangulated terrain mesh in a bounding-volume hierarchy, and is reported as roughly two orders of magnitude faster than a conventional DEM walk for that workload. That result is about computing horizons, not about radar cross-section. — [HORAYZON preprint, GMD 2022](https://gmd.copernicus.org/preprints/gmd-2022-58/gmd-2022-58-ATC2.pdf)
- The missilesim flight harness places a vehicle at `glm::vec3(0, altitude, 0)`, so altitude is the y component of a local Cartesian frame in that tool. — `tools/flight_harness/main.cpp` in this repository

### Inferences

- For a theater a few hundred kilometres across, the P.526 / GDAL parabolic bulge on a sphere of radius a_e is the right earth model. WGS84’s equatorial and polar radii differ by about 21 km, and sqrt(a) scaling means the horizon changes by a fraction of a percent. Measured k varies by tens of percent (the RF Essentials range from subrefraction through ducting). Refraction uncertainty dominates ellipsoid flattening. Use WGS84 only if the integrator is already ECEF and the same Cartesian frame must serve navigation and the ray.
- Vertical datum must be one datum. If aircraft altitudes and the DEM are both orthometric, bulge them together. Do not subtract a DEM of orthometric heights from an ellipsoidal ray without the geoid.
- Grazing angle at a surface point, from the law of cosines on the same sphere (radar at radius a_e + h, surface point at radius a_e, slant range R): sin ψ = (2 a_e h + h² − R²) / (2 a_e R). It is zero on the radio horizon, where R = sqrt(2 a_e h + h²). Flat-earth limit for small h is ψ ≈ h/R − R/(2 a_e), in radians. A local terrain slope α along the line of sight changes the grazing angle that clutter and reflection actually see to about ψ − α. Ignoring slope keeps the mask, the reflection angle, and σ0 all slightly wrong, and wrong in the same direction on a facing slope.
- Sampling: the first Fresnel radius at X-band (λ = 0.03 m) at the midpoint of a 40 km path is sqrt(λ d/4) = sqrt(300) ≈ 17 m, calculated from the P.526 F1 formula. A 30–90 m DEM will not resolve that zone, but it will resolve the ridges that decide masking. Vertical error of a few metres matters more than horizontal posting once the posting is finer than the ridge. Building and tree heights are a separate layer; a bare-earth DEM will show line of sight through towns.
- Runtime test for one target is a DDA or fixed-step walk on the ground track, not a full viewshed. Full viewsheds and horizon skylines are precomputes for fixed sites.

## Knife-edge and rounded-obstacle diffraction

### Takeaway

A ridge that cuts the geometric ray does not reduce the field to zero. ITU-R P.526 collapses the geometry into one dimensionless Fresnel parameter ν and a closed-form loss J(ν) that is about 6 dB when the edge is exactly on the ray and grows thereafter. Computing J at the single worst point on an already sampled profile is a few flops. A boolean mask is the wrong model within a few decibels of grazing the edge; the full multiple-cylinder procedure is the wrong model for a per-dwell budget.

### Cited Findings

- For a single knife edge, with h the signed height of the obstacle above the straight line joining the terminals (negative if the edge is below the line), d1 and d2 the distances from the edge to each terminal, λ the wavelength, and θ the diffraction angle in radians (same sign as h, and the small-angle form assumes |θ| less than about 0.2 rad): ν = h · sqrt( (2/λ) (1/d1 + 1/d2) ). Equivalent forms in the same recommendation: ν = θ · sqrt( (2/λ) (1/d1 + 1/d2) ), ν = sqrt(2 h θ / λ), and ν = sqrt(2 d / λ) · α1 α2, with ν taking the sign of h. — [ITU-R P.526-5 (1997) PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.526-5-199708-S!!PDF-E.pdf)
- The 1997 text gives, for ν greater than −0.7, J(ν) = 6.9 + 20 log10( sqrt( (ν − 0.1)² + 1 ) + ν − 0.1 ) dB. — [ITU-R P.526-5 (1997) PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.526-5-199708-S!!PDF-E.pdf)
- A later revision, as cited from P.526-14, writes ν = h · sqrt( 2 (d1 + d2) / (λ d1 d2) ), which is algebraically the same as the 1997 square-root form, and uses J(ν) = 6.9 + 20 log10( sqrt( (ν − 0.1)² + 1 ) + ν − 0.1 ) for ν > −0.78, and J = 0 for ν ≤ −0.78. The diffraction zone is defined there as starting where clearance falls to 60% of the first Fresnel radius. ν = −0.78 is 55% of that radius, because clearance fraction = |ν| / sqrt(2) when the obstacle is below the ray. — [CalcEngines Fresnel page, citing P.526-14](https://calcengines.com/electronics/rf-antennas/fresnel-zone-calculator/)
- That secondary table compares the approximation with the Fresnel integral: at ν = 0 both are about 6.0–6.1 dB; at ν = 0.707 about 11.9 dB; at ν = 1.414 about 16.2–16.3 dB; at 60% clearance the approximation is 0 dB while the integral still shows a fraction of a decibel. The approximation deliberately drops the sub-decibel ripple in the lit region. — [CalcEngines Fresnel page](https://calcengines.com/electronics/rf-antennas/fresnel-zone-calculator/)
- When the knife edge is on the line of sight (ν = 0), the field is half the free-space field: 6.02 dB of attenuation and no phase shift. Deeper obstruction increases attenuation monotonically. — [Barclay, Propagation of Radiowaves, as extracted](https://vdoc.pub/documents/propagation-of-radiowaves-6tpa98vjjlm0)
- P.526-16 §4.2 is a single rounded obstacle, §4.3 double isolated edges, §4.4 multiple isolated cylinders, and §4.5 a general terrestrial path with a Bullington model and a complete method. The 2025 text still reduces the worst sub-path obstruction to ν = −C_F sqrt(2) and then to the knife-edge loss. An isolated cylinder radius is smoothed for marginally line-of-sight cases so the rounded term does not jump. — [ITU-R P.526-16 (2025) PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.526-16-202511-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- Spherical-earth diffraction, as opposed to an isolated edge, is a separate section of the same recommendation (§3), including a numerical method and nomograms. A thesis on radar propagation states that below a limiting grazing angle a ray construction should be replaced by spherical-earth diffraction. The OCR of the limit formula in that thesis is ambiguous and is not quoted. — [ITU-R P.526-16](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.526-16-202511-I!!PDF-E.pdf); [de Arriba Ruiz, UPM](https://oa.upm.es/14258/1/IMANOL_DE_ARRIBA_RUIZ.pdf)

### Inferences

- J(ν) is a one-way loss. At ν = 0 the field amplitude is 1/2, so one-way power is 1/4, and 10 log10(4) = 6 dB. Field decibels and power decibels match because 20 log10(E/E0) = 10 log10(P/P0). For a monostatic radar the wave passes the same obstacle twice. If F is the one-way field factor, received power scales as |F|⁴, so the two-way power loss in dB is 2 J(ν). That doubling is radar reciprocity applied to a one-way ITU formula, not a sentence in P.526.
- Boolean mask versus J(ν): the approximation is already 0 dB for clearance beyond about 0.78 of a Fresnel radius (ν ≤ −0.78) and is only ~6 dB with the edge on the ray. A hard cull at the geometric line throws away targets that are still within a few decibels of free space, and it creates a pop as a missile crests a ridge. Evaluating one log for the worst sample removes that discontinuity. Targets many Fresnel radii into the shadow (ν of several units) are tens of decibels down and can be culled.
- Frame budget: the profile walk is O(samples) and is the cost. J(ν) is closed form. Bullington (§4.5.1) collapses a whole profile to one equivalent edge and is the cheap general-path method if a walk must be shortened. Rounded-crest loss and the multiple-cylinder “complete method” are justified offline, or for a fixed ground site whose horizon is baked, not inside a per-pulse loop. Do not run a Fresnel integral per dwell; P.526 already replaced it with J(ν).
- Use the P.526-14 threshold ν = −0.78 rather than the 1997 value −0.7 unless the project pins an older edition. The two editions agree on the algebraic expression for J. The exact integral’s ±1 dB ripple above the lit region is below other errors in σ0 and in k.
- The earth bulge itself is an obstacle. Putting h_bulge = d1 d2 / (2 a_e) into the profile and then calling J(ν) is the sub-path method P.526 already specifies. It is the smooth-earth diffraction a sim should use past the radio horizon. The residue-series spherical-earth model in §3 is the reference solution, not the runtime one.

## Multipath over land and sea

### Takeaway

Over a smooth surface the target is illuminated by a direct ray and one specular reflection. The classic path difference is Δd = sqrt((h_t + h_r)² + d²) − sqrt((h_t − h_r)² + d²) ≈ 2 h_t h_r / d, and the phase is 4π h_t h_r / (λ d). For a monostatic radar the free-space power is multiplied by |F|⁴, not by the one-way two-ray path-loss formula. That product is altitude lobing: deep nulls at regular heights, peaks up to +12 dB on received power, and an R⁻⁸ decay in the region below the first lobe. Horizontal and vertical polarization share |Γ| → 1 at grazing but differ through the Brewster region. Rough or sloped ground replaces the single image with a weaker specular coefficient plus a diffuse fill that keeps nulls from going to zero.

### Cited Findings

- Exact flat-earth path lengths: reflected path x + x′ = sqrt((h_t + h_r)² + d²), direct path l = sqrt((h_t − h_r)² + d²), so Δd = sqrt((h_t + h_r)² + d²) − sqrt((h_t − h_r)² + d²). Phase difference Δφ = 2π Δd / λ. For d ≫ (h_t + h_r), Δd ≈ 2 h_t h_r / d and Δφ ≈ 4π h_t h_r / (λ d). — [Wikipedia, Two-ray ground-reflection model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_reflection)
- Voltage reflection coefficient at grazing angle θ, relative permittivity ε_g: Γ(θ) = (sin θ − X) / (sin θ + X), with X_h = sqrt(ε_g − cos² θ) for horizontal polarization and X_v = X_h / ε_g for vertical. At large d, Γ → −1 for the case the article plots. Received power of a one-way link then goes as P_r ≈ P_t G h_t² h_r² / d⁴, i.e. 40 dB/decade, valid when Δφ ≪ 1, equivalently d ≫ 4π h_t h_r / λ. The article states that this far-field formula fails when the heights are not small compared with the range, and when the ground is not an ideal plane; Γ ≈ −1 is then unjustified. — [Wikipedia, Two-ray ground-reflection model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_reflection)
- A lecture note evaluates the Fresnel formulas at θ = 0 for a lossless dielectric and gets |R| = 1 for both polarizations, with the perpendicular coefficient equal to −1. The parallel-coefficient sign at exactly θ = 0 depends on the Fresnel sign convention used in that note (they print +1). The Wikipedia form above gives Γ → −1 for both as θ → 0 because both X_h and X_v stay positive. — [MMMUT lecture notes](https://www.mmmut.ac.in/News_content/14053dep-notice_04292020.pdf); [Wikipedia, Two-ray model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_reflection)
- For the monostatic radar under the assumptions Γ = −1 and equal antenna gains on the two rays, Mahafza gives F² = 4 sin²(ΔΦ / 2) = 4 sin²(2π h_t h_r / (λ R)), then by reciprocity F⁴ = 16 sin⁴(2π h_t h_r / (λ R)). Received power is the free-space radar return multiplied by that F⁴, so it swings between 0 and 16 times free space. Nulls satisfy 2 h_r h_t / (λ R) = n for integer n. Peak target heights are given as h_t = n λ R / (4 h_r) for positive integers n. In the small-angle limit the same derivation replaces the range dependence by R⁻⁸: P_r proportional to (h_t h_r)⁴ / (λ² R⁸). — [Mahafza, Radar Systems Analysis and Design Using MATLAB, ch. 8, as extracted](https://dsp-book.narod.ru/RSAD/C1828_PDF_C08.pdf)
- Blake’s definition, quoted in a later radar-equation treatment: the pattern-propagation factor is the ratio of the electric-field amplitude at a point to the free-space amplitude with the beam aimed at that point. It includes the elevation pattern and surface reflection, and for low-elevation paths it also includes diffraction. The radar equation carries F_t and F_r; for a monostatic reciprocal path the power factor is F_t² F_r². Atmospheric absorption is kept as a separate loss, not inside F. — [Blake, NRL report excerpt AD0701321](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/AD0701321.pdf); [Barton, Radar System Analysis and Modeling, preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781580536820_A25904218/preview-9781580536820_A25904218.pdf)
- An NRL plotting report writes the flat-earth pattern-propagation factor as F = f(θ1) |1 + X exp(jψ)|, where X folds the reflection coefficient with the antenna pattern on the reflected ray, and identifies the lobe structure with Lloyd’s mirror. The report’s contents list separate sections for flat-earth reflection, a rough-surface reflection coefficient, and spherical-earth reflection. — [Blake, NRL Report 7098](https://users.monash.edu/~annn/shadowfax/FIT1016-2044-2012/Blake-NRL-AD-709-897-Machine-Plotting-1970-A.pdf)
- Barton's propagation discussion, as extracted: lobing at short range decays because the specular scattering coefficient falls as grazing angle increases. On a smooth surface without vegetation the lobes continue to peak at +6 dB (one-way field factor of 2, since 20 log10(2) = 6) until the antenna pattern rolls off. — [Barton text extract](https://picture.iczhiku.com/resource/eetop/WhKGWITppyUkeCCX.pdf)
- Rayleigh roughness geometry in an open thesis: path difference between rays reflected from a surface excursion h_σ is ΔR = 2 h_σ sin ψ, and Δφ = 4π h_σ sin ψ / λ. A very small phase difference is treated as a smooth, specular surface. The same thesis says a ray model should be abandoned below a limiting grazing angle in favor of spherical-earth diffraction; the printed limit is too corrupted to quote. — [de Arriba Ruiz, UPM](https://oa.upm.es/14258/1/IMANOL_DE_ARRIBA_RUIZ.pdf)
- MathWorks’ two-ray geometry for a flat boundary: R_los = sqrt((z_r − z_s)² + L²), R_reflected = sqrt((z_r + z_s)² + L²), with L the ground range. Reflection angle follows from the image. They note that a flat-surface assumption is the short-range model (cellular, automotive) and that the reflection loss may be an angle-dependent input rather than a single constant. — [MathWorks, Two-Ray Multipath Propagation](https://www.mathworks.com/help/phased/ug/two-ray-multipath-propagation.html)

### Inferences

- Do not drop the communications formula P ∝ 1/d⁴ into a radar that already has free-space R⁻⁴. That one-way far-field law is the small-angle limit of |1 − exp(−jΔφ)|² on a single pass. The radar sees that factor on the way out and again on the way back, which is the R⁻⁸ region Mahafza gives, and only below the first lobe. Inside the lobing region use 16 sin⁴(2π h_r h_t / (λ R)), or the complex form |1 + Γ exp(−jΔφ)|⁴ if Γ is not −1.
- Nulls of sin(2π h_r h_t / (λ R)) are at h_t = n λ R / (2 h_r). Maxima are at h_t = (2n+1) λ R / (4 h_r). The Mahafza index “n = 1, 2, …” on h_t = n λ R / (4 h_r) includes the even values, which are nulls. Use the odd subset. Spacing between successive nulls at fixed range is Δh = λ R / (2 h_r).
- Calculated example, not a measurement: λ = 0.03 m (10 GHz), antenna height 15 m, range 20 km. First lobe peak at h_t = λ R / (4 h_r) = 10 m. A missile at 5 m is halfway down that lobe in height. At 40 km the same peak is at 20 m, so the 5 m missile has moved toward the null. Constant-altitude flight therefore fades in range as well as in height. That is altitude lobing.
- Vertical polarization has a Brewster point where sin θ = X_v and Γ_v = 0, so the nulls fill in at medium grazing angles. At true grazing both polarizations approach |Γ| = 1 with a sign that cancels the field on the surface, which is why a target at h = 0 is a null on a smooth sea. Forcing Γ = −1 at a 20° look-down is a horizontal-polarization model, not a vertical one.
- Setting the thesis phase Δφ = π/2 in Δφ = 4π h_σ sin ψ / λ gives the usual Rayleigh threshold h_σ = λ / (8 sin ψ). That threshold is this derivation plus a π/2 cutoff; it was not copied from a sentence that says “λ/8”. The exponential roughness factor that multiplies Γ has more than one published constant (Ament versus Miller-Brown). It is not quoted here.
- Apparent sea clutter already folds in low-angle multipath (see the next section). Applying |F|⁴ to the target and also using an “apparent” σ0 for the sea is correct only if the clutter model was intrinsic. Mixing GIT or Nathanson apparent σ0 with a second |F|⁴ double-counts the surface field.

### Gaps

- No opened table of complex permittivity of sea water versus frequency (ITU-R P.527 was not opened). ε_g in the Fresnel formula should be complex; a real dielectric misses the phase away from grazing. Leave ε as data.
- The numerical specular-reduction factor ρ_s(σ_h, ψ, λ) was not pinned to one recommendation. P.526’s rough-surface section titles exist in Blake’s report contents, but the formula was not extracted.
- Diffuse (incoherent) power that fills nulls is described qualitatively by Barton’s decaying lobes. No diffuse-scattering cross-section coefficient was opened.
- Spherical-earth image location (the reflection point is not at the flat-earth height ratio once the bulge matters) is named as a Blake section and is not given as a closed form here. Iterate the point where the two grazing angles match on the local tangent.

## Surface clutter

### Takeaway

Look-down is clutter-limited because the competing cross-section is σ0 times a patch that is kilometres wide, not because the thermal noise rises. The patch is pulse-limited at low grazing angle: area ≈ R θ_az (c τ / 2) / cos ψ. σ0 itself is γ sin ψ for a constant-gamma land or sea class, with γ taken from a published table, or a sea-state model that was fit to Nathanson’s measurements. Low-grazing sea models disagree with those measurements by about 10 dB, and several of them already include propagation, so they must not be stacked blindly on the multipath factor.

### Cited Findings

- Normalized reflectivity σ0 is RCS per unit area (m²/m²). Constant-gamma form used by MathWorks: σ0 = 10^(γ_dB / 10) · sin ψ, with ψ the grazing angle. Default γ = −40 dB is described as representative of sea state 3. — [MathWorks, surfaceReflectivitySea](https://www.mathworks.com/help/radar/ref/surfacereflectivitysea-system-object.html)
- Gamma values at a 10 GHz reference, stated as derived from measurements: sea state 3, −40 dB; sea state 5, −30 dB; woods, −15 dB; metropolitan, 0 dB; rugged mountain, 0 dB; farmland, −15 dB; wooded hill, −10 dB; flatland, −20 dB. Farmland, wooded hill, and flatland are attributed to a second source in that documentation. ### Inferences

- Why look-down is clutter, not noise. Pulse-limited σ_c = σ0 A with A ∝ R, so clutter power in the radar equation falls as R⁻³ while a point target falls as R⁻⁴. At short and medium range the cell is clutter-limited if σ_c exceeds the noise-equivalent RCS at that range. The noise-equivalent RCS belongs to the detection note; this note only supplies σ_c.
- Calculated illustration, constant-gamma, not a measurement. Farmland γ = −15 dB, ψ = 5°, so σ0 = 10^(−1.5) sin 5° = 8.72×10⁻⁴ (−30.6 dB). With R = 20 km, θ_az = 2° = 0.035 rad, τ = 1 μs so c τ / 2 = 150 m, and sec 5° ≈ 1.004: A ≈ 20 000 × 0.035 × 150 × 1.004 ≈ 1.05×10⁵ m². σ_c ≈ 92 m² ≈ 20 dBsm. A 1 m² aircraft in that cell is about 20 dB below the clutter mean before any processing gain. Metropolitan γ = 0 dB raises σ0, and therefore σ_c, by 15 dB relative to farmland. Sea state 3 at γ = −40 dB lowers it by 25 dB relative to farmland, which is why a calm sea can look noise-limited in a geometry where farmland does not.
- Use the pulse-limited area whenever (c τ / 2) / cos ψ is smaller than the elevation footprint R θ_el / sin ψ. Fighter look-down at a few degrees almost always is pulse-limited for a pencil beam. A wide fan beam or a long uncompressed pulse can be beam-limited in elevation and still pulse-limited in a narrow azimuth.
- If the waveform is pulse-compressed, the length that enters A is c / (2 B), not the transmitted pulse width. The cited formulas say τ because they assume a simple pulse.
- Coding choice for missilesim: constant-gamma with the Long/MathWorks class table is the runtime model. It is defined, cheap, and separable from multipath (Barton applies propagation on top of sea gamma). Do not use Morchin below about 70° grazing. Do not use GIT land outside 20°–65°. For sea, constant-gamma at −40 dB (sea state 3) or −30 dB (sea state 5) is the consistent partner to a separate |F|⁴. If a later table lookup uses Nathanson or the NRL coefficients, turn the extra propagation factor off or the clutter will be counted twice. GIT’s 10 dB low bias at low sea state is large enough to change a detection call; do not prefer GIT over Nathanson/NRL for fidelity. The NRL algebra should be copied from the DTIC PDF, not reconstructed from the broken line break.
- σ0 is a mean. Billingsley’s low-angle land-clutter work, cited by secondary sources, is about the Weibull spread around that mean. No Weibull parameters were extracted. A detection note that needs Pfa in land clutter cannot assume Rayleigh speckle on a fixed σ0 without that spread.

### Gaps

- Individual cells of Nathanson’s sea and land tables were not copied. They are a copyrighted book. Only the model-to-table errors above are from open memoranda.
- The NRL closed-form expression is intentionally not retyped. Coefficients are quoted; operators between them need the PDF.
- No urban discrete-scatterer RCS (a building, a mast) was opened. Homogeneous σ0 under-represents a town, which is Barton’s grazing-incidence gap. A class label “urban” with γ = 0 dB is a floor, not a building model.
- Wind-direction dependence of sea clutter (upwind versus crosswind) is acknowledged by the DSTO note and not quantified here beyond “upwind is the brightest cut of the NRL form.”

## Weather and clouds

### Takeaway

Rain enters the detection equation only as a specific attenuation in dB/km and as a volumetric cross-section. ITU-R P.838 gives γ_R = k R^α. The Marshall-Palmer relation Z = 200 R^1.6 converts rain rate to a reflectivity factor; turning Z into η (m²/m³) is a standard meteorological constant that was not on the pages opened here. Non-precipitating cloud is a separate, smaller term and no coefficient for it was opened.

### Cited Findings

- Specific attenuation (dB/km) is γ_R = k R^α, with R the rain rate in mm/h. Polarization interpolation in the 1992 recommendation: k = [k_H + k_V + (k_H − k_V) cos² θ cos 2τ] / 2, and α = [k_H α_H + k_V α_V + (k_H α_H − k_V α_V) cos² θ cos 2τ] / (2 k), where θ is path elevation and τ is polarization tilt from horizontal (τ = 45° for circular). — [ITU-R P.838-0 (1992) PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.838-0-199203-S!!PDF-E.pdf)
- P.838-0 Table 1, horizontal-path regression coefficients (standard, since superseded — see below). Selected rows, (frequency GHz, k_H, α_H, k_V, α_V): 1, 3.87×10⁻⁵, 0.912, 3.52×10⁻⁵, 0.880; 4, 6.50×10⁻⁴, 1.121, 5.91×10⁻⁴, 1.075; 8, 4.54×10⁻³, 1.327, 3.95×10⁻³, 1.310; 10, 0.0101, 1.276, 0.00887, 1.264; 15, 0.0367, 1.154, 0.0335, 1.128; 35, 0.263, 0.979, 0.233, 0.963. The recommendation’s table continues to 100 GHz. — [ITU-R P.838-0 (1992) PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.838-0-199203-S!!PDF-E.pdf)
- P.838-3 (2005) is the version implemented by current open libraries. It replaces the table with a log-frequency regression valid from 1 to 1000 GHz and keeps γ_R = k R^α. A 2026 measurement paper quotes that model, for 45° elevation and circular polarization, as a = 0.094, b = 1.020 at 20 GHz, against the 1992 horizontal-path k_H(20 GHz) = 0.0751. The versions are not interchangeable at the percent level. — [ITU-Rpy P.838](https://itu-rpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/apidoc/itu838.html); [Hong et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/sat.70016); [P.838-3 PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/r-rec-p.838-3-200503-i!!pdf-e.pdf)
- Marshall and Palmer’s widely used relation is Z = 200 R^1.6, with Z in mm⁶ m⁻³ and R in mm h⁻¹. NWS history: the WSR-57 network used that relation; the WSR-88D network changed the operational default to Z = 300 R^1.4. A 2025 Delhi comparison still finds Marshall-Palmer the better of three tested relations on that site’s overall set, and finds the ranking changes with season and intensity. The relation is empirical and not unique. — [NWS Tallahassee, Z-R note](https://www.weather.gov/tae/research-zrpaper); [ScienceDirect Delhi Z-R comparison, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1474706525003328)
- Other empirical Z-R forms printed by Radartutorial: stratiform rain Z = 200 R^1.6; orographic Z = 31 R^1.71; convective cloud Z = 286 R^1.37; snow Z = 2000 R². The same page notes that a and b are time averages and that an instantaneous spectrum can depart from them a great deal. — [Radartutorial, reflectivity (French)](https://www.radartutorial.eu/15.weather/fr/wr50.fr.html)
- A hydrology text records that Marshall and Palmer’s 1948 paper gave Z = 220 R^1.6, and that Z = 200 R^1.6 is the later slight revision (Marshall et al., 1955). Drop diameters used for the original fit were about 1.0 to 3.5 mm. — [Hydrology and Floodplain Analysis, 5th ed., as extracted](https://dokumen.pub/hydrology-and-floodplain-analysis-5th-ed-9780132567961-0273774271-9780273774273-0132567962.html)
- Barton states that rain attenuation in decibels varies as f⁴, and that backscatter from rain and other small particles also varies as f⁴ over most of the microwave region. The f⁴ backscatter is the Rayleigh λ⁻⁴ inside η, not a second attenuation law. — [Barton, Radar System Analysis and Modeling, preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781580536820_A25904218/preview-9781580536820_A25904218.pdf)

### Inferences

- Interface, and only the interface. Two-way rain loss in dB is L_rain = 2 γ_R D_rain, where D_rain is the path length inside rain in km, not necessarily the whole slant range. γ_R comes from P.838-3 (preferred) or, for a checked X-band approximation, from the 1992 row at 8–10 GHz. Calculated from the 1992 10 GHz horizontal row only: k R^α at R = 10 mm/h is 0.0101 × 10^1.276 ≈ 0.191 dB/km one way, so 11 dB two-way over 30 km of that rain. At 4 GHz the same rain is about 0.009 dB/km one way (k_H = 6.5×10⁻⁴, α_H = 1.121), which is negligible at fighter ranges. At 35 GHz, k_H = 0.263 and α_H = 0.979, so 10 mm/h is about 2.5 dB/km one way. Label these three as arithmetic on P.838-0, not as P.838-3 output.
- Weather clutter cross-section is σ_w = η V, where V is the resolution volume (approximately R² θ_az θ_el · (c τ / 2) for a pencil beam that is filled by rain) and η is the volume reflectivity in m²/m³. Z = 200 R^1.6 is the rule-of-thumb reflectivity factor to feed that conversion for stratiform rain. Snow and convective rain should use a different (a, b), not the same 200 and 1.6.
- Because both attenuation and η depend on R very steeply, a single “rain on/off” flag is a poor model. A rain-rate number in mm/h, even if it is uniform along the beam, is the data the formulas actually take.
- Clouds without rain were not given a coefficient. They should not be assigned the rain Z-R. If the sim needs a cloud return, it needs a separate η_cloud, which this pass does not have.

### Gaps

- The factor η = (π⁵ / λ⁴) |K|² Z × 10⁻¹⁸, and the value |K|² ≈ 0.93 for liquid water, were not present on an opened page. Do not hard-code them until Doviak and Zrnić or Battan is checked. The detection equation can take σ_w once that factor is confirmed.
- ITU-R P.676 (oxygen and water vapor) was not opened. No gaseous dB/km number is stated. At X-band it is a small companion to heavy-rain P.838 loss; that comparison is not quantified here.
- ITU-R P.840 cloud attenuation was not opened.
- P.838-3’s regression coefficients (the a_j, b_j, c_j tables) were not copied. The 1992 table is quoted because that PDF text was readable. Implementation should prefer P.838-3.

## Ground effect on low-altitude aircraft and missiles

### Takeaway

The flat-earth image antenna is the construction behind the two-ray formulas: a reflected path of length sqrt((h_t + h_r)² + d²) is exactly the path from an image at −h. It is the right model for a smooth sea or a flat field inside the first tens of kilometres, and it is the reason a sea-skimming missile sits in a fading lobe structure rather than in free space. It fails as soon as the surface slope, the earth bulge, or roughness moves the specular point, and it fails completely past the radio horizon, where the path is diffraction, not a reflection.

### Cited Findings

- The two-ray geometry is the image construction: the reflected length is the distance to an image antenna as far below the plane as the real antenna is above it. The article warns that the Γ ≈ −1, d⁻⁴ result does not hold when the ground cannot be modelled as an ideal plane, which is the usual case for airborne nodes at medium and high altitude, and that the required fade margin then changes. — [Wikipedia, Two-ray ground-reflection model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_reflection)
- Blake’s coverage-diagram report treats flat-earth reflection, rough-surface reflection, and spherical-earth reflection as three successive corrections, and treats refraction by changing the earth radius used to draw the lobes. — [Blake, NRL Report 7098](https://users.monash.edu/~annn/shadowfax/FIT1016-2044-2012/Blake-NRL-AD-709-897-Machine-Plotting-1970-A.pdf)
- The pattern-propagation factor on low-elevation paths includes diffraction as well as reflection. Past the horizon the image ray is not the propagation mechanism. — [Barton preview, quoting Blake](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781580536820_A25904218/preview-9781580536820_A25904218.pdf); [ITU-R P.526-16 §3](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.526-16-202511-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- MathWorks states that the effective-earth-radius method ignores ducting. A k-factor image, however carefully height-corrected, does not produce a surface duct. — [MathWorks, effearthradius](https://www.mathworks.com/help/releases/R2024a/radar/ref/effearthradius.html)

### Inferences

- What a slope breaks, concretely. On a tilted facet the image is mirrored through the facet, not through the horizontal plane. The specular point moves, the grazing angle used in Γ and in σ0 changes by about the slope angle, and a slope that faces away can eliminate the specular point entirely (the bounce is shadowed). A sim that places the image at (x, −h) over a hillside will both invent a reflection that does not exist and miss the reflection off the facing slope. Test for a specular point on the local tangent; if none lies on the visible profile, set the coherent Γ path to zero and keep only the direct ray, still multiplied by knife-edge loss if the direct ray is cut.
- Curvature breaks the same image more quietly. The bulge d² / (2 a_e) at 30 km is d² / (2 × 8.49×10⁶) ≈ 53 m, calculated with a_e = 4/3 × 6371 km. An image reflected in the horizontal tangent at the radar is already tens of metres off the sea at that range. Use the spherical grazing angle from the line-of-sight section, or stop the coherent sum once the specular point would fall past the radio horizon.
- Low-altitude missiles are the small-angle case. With Γ ≈ −1, |F|⁴ = 16 sin⁴(2π h_r h_t / (λ R)) ≈ 16 (2π h_r h_t / (λ R))⁴ when the missile is well below the first lobe. Free-space detection range is then replaced by a much shorter range, and the dependence on both heights is fourth power. Pulling the missile down from 20 m to 5 m is a factor of 4⁴ = 256 in power (−24 dB) inside that approximation, calculated from Mahafza’s small-angle form, until the first lobe or a duct is reached. This is the propagation reason low-altitude ingress works against a smooth-earth radar. It is not a guidance technique; it is the |F|⁴ factor the detection equation should see.
- Roughness and vegetation do the opposite of a perfect image: they reduce the coherent lobe peak toward 0 dB and fill the nulls. A sim that uses Γ = −1 over land with the Long “woods” or “metropolitan” class will invent nulls the clutter model says are not there. Tie the coherent sum to the smooth classes (sea, flatland) and taper it with the Rayleigh phase 4π σ_h sin ψ / λ from the multipath section.
- Ground radars and airborne radars need the same function. Only h_r changes. A mast height of 15 m versus a radar at 3 km moves the first lobe from tens of metres of target altitude to hundreds of metres at the same range, because the peak height scales as 1/h_r.

### Gaps

- No quantitative “how many decibels does a 5° slope cost” measurement was opened. The failure mode is geometric and certain; the decibel figure for a particular hillside is a profile calculation, not a tabulated constant.
- Ducting, which can restore a low target beyond the 4/3 horizon, has no runtime model in the sources opened beyond “k-factor does not do this.”

## What runtime data a sim needs

### Takeaway

The live state is already in the sim: positions, altitudes, and the radar’s frequency, polarization, beamwidths, and range resolution. The data that must be loaded are an elevation grid, a surface class, a roughness or sea state, and a refraction choice that defaults to k = 4/3. Precompute horizon skylines for fixed radars. Solve one profile, one Fresnel J(ν), and one two-ray factor per radar-target dwell, not per pulse and not as a full viewshed.

### Cited Findings

- Effective-earth default: refgrad = −39×10⁻⁹ m⁻¹, giving k ≈ 1.33. The method does not include ducting. An altitude-dependent effective radius is available if both heights and the slant range are known, and the sample values at 8–9 km target altitude are several percent below 4/3. — [MathWorks, effearthradius](https://www.mathworks.com/help/releases/R2024a/radar/ref/effearthradius.html)
- A viewshed over a DEM is defined for a projected metre grid, with explicit observer and target heights, an optional curvature coefficient, and an optional maximum range. Cumulative observers are a separate, heavier mode. — [GDAL gdal_viewshed](https://gdal.org/en/stable/programs/gdal_viewshed.html)
- Horizon-in-azimuth can be reduced to a few hundred rays over a mesh, and that reduction is what makes a full-skyline precompute practical. — [HORAYZON preprint](https://gmd.copernicus.org/preprints/gmd-2022-58/gmd-2022-58-ATC2.pdf)
- Surface class enters through the gamma table (sea state, woods, urban, mountain, farmland, flatland) or through a model whose validity is limited in grazing angle and frequency (GIT land, Morchin, Nathanson). — [MathWorks, surfacegamma](https://www.mathworks.com/help/radar/ref/surfacegamma.html); [MathWorks, surfaceReflectivityLand](https://uk.mathworks.com/help/releases/R2025b/radar/ref/surfacereflectivityland-system-object.html)
- Rain rate in mm/h is the sole meteorological input of P.838 and of Z = 200 R^1.6. — [ITU-R P.838-0](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.838-0-199203-S!!PDF-E.pdf); [NWS Z-R note](https://www.weather.gov/tae/research-zrpaper)
- The profile method needs the effective radius, the wavelength, and the terrain heights along the path, and it returns a clearance and a ν. — [ITU-R P.526-16](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/p/R-REC-P.526-16-202511-I!!PDF-E.pdf)

### Inferences

- Minimum data products, all on the same vertical datum as aircraft altitude:
  1. Elevation raster in projected metres. Posting of a few tens of metres is enough for ridge masking; it is not enough for the X-band Fresnel zone (~17 m at 40 km, calculated earlier).
  2. Surface class per cell, mapped to γ: at least sea, flatland, farmland, woods, urban, mountain. Sea needs a sea state (default 3, γ = −40 dB) rather than a land gamma.
  3. Roughness σ_h in metres, or a default per class (calm sea small, woods large). Used only to decide whether the coherent reflection is active.
  4. Refraction: a scalar k, default 4/3, or dn/dh in m⁻¹. Not a 3-D refractivity field unless ducting is added later.
  5. Optional rain rate in mm/h, uniform or a coarse 3-D field. Optional snow/convective flag to pick a different Z-R pair.
- Do not load: classified clutter maps, a global σ0 raster that already bakes in one radar’s frequency and grazing angle, or a precomputed visibility mask for every aircraft altitude. σ0 depends on ψ, which depends on the radar’s height.
- Precompute versus per dwell:
  - Fixed ground radar: horizon skyline, the maximum terrain elevation angle in each azimuth bin (1° is enough), stored with the site and invalidated if k changes. A target below the skyline and below the smooth-earth horizon is a diffraction case; a target above the skyline is clear of terrain and only the multipath and the weather remain.
  - Moving airborne radar: do not viewshed the whole map per frame. Keep a coarse skyline in azimuth and refresh it when the platform moves by on the order of a DEM cell, or ray-march only the azimuth of each live target.
  - Per dwell (or a few hertz per track, not per PRI): ground range and slant range from the two position vectors; smooth-earth horizon test; a few dozen to a few hundred DEM samples along the track; one ν and one J(ν); one specular point and one |F|⁴ if the surface class is smooth and the specular point is visible; one ψ, one σ0, one cell area, one σ_c if the beam’s lower edge meets the surface; one γ_R if rain rate is nonzero.
- Memory shape that matches the current harness: positions are y-up Cartesian metres in the flight tools. Curvature is a correction on the profile, Height_effective = y − d1 d2 / (2 a_e) for the ray, or the P.526 form that adds the bulge to the terrain. It does not require moving the integrator to ECEF.

### Gaps

- No measurement of an acceptable DEM vertical RMSE for this sim was opened. A few metres was inferred from lobe spacing and from the bulge, not from a flight-test reference.
- Land-cover crosswalks (which map class becomes “woods” versus “farmland”) were not opened. The gamma table is useless until that legend exists.
- P.453 maps of the refractivity gradient were not opened, so a geographic k(lat, lon, month) is not available to recommend.

## Implementation notes for missilesim

### Takeaway

Per radar-target dwell, turn the two position vectors and their altitudes into a two-way diffraction loss, a multipath power factor |F|⁴, a two-way rain loss, and one clutter cross-section. Cull on a deep diffraction loss only. Do not cull on a flat-earth infinite horizon, and do not apply the communications d⁻⁴ law on top of the radar equation.

### Algorithm

Symbols below are metres, radians, and hertz unless noted. a_e = k R_e with R_e = 6.371×10⁶ m and k = 4/3 (or k = 1 / (1 + R_e · dn/dh) if a gradient is stored). c = 2.99792458×10⁸ m/s. Heights h_r and h_t are above the same datum as the DEM, in the y-up frame the flight harness already uses (`position.y`).

1. Slant range R and ground range L from the two positions. If the world is still flat Cartesian, L = horizontal distance and the bulge is applied only inside the profile, which is the GDAL and P.526 approach.
2. Smooth-earth horizon. d_r = sqrt(2 a_e h_r + h_r²), d_t = sqrt(2 a_e h_t + h_t²). If R > d_r + d_t the geometric ray is below the smooth horizon. Continue; do not return “invisible” yet.
3. Profile. Step along L at about the DEM posting. At fraction u (distance d_u from the radar, d_v = L − d_u):
   - h_ray = [(h_r) d_v + (h_t) d_u] / L
   - h_obs = z_DEM + d_u d_v / (2 a_e)
   - clearance c_m = h_ray − h_obs
   - F1 = sqrt(λ d_u d_v / L), ν = −sqrt(2) c_m / F1
   Keep the maximum ν. This is P.526-16’s sub-path parameter.
4. Diffraction loss, one-way, in dB. If ν ≤ −0.78, J = 0. Otherwise J = 6.9 + 20 log10( sqrt((ν − 0.1)² + 1) + ν − 0.1 ). Two-way power loss L_diff = 2 J. If L_diff exceeds a cull threshold around 40 dB, drop the track and skip the rest. The threshold is a frame-budget rule of thumb, not a physical constant.
5. Multipath, only if L_diff = 0 and the class under the specular point is smooth (sea or flatland) and that point is itself visible from both ends. Flat specular point divides L in the ratio of the heights. Path difference Δd = sqrt((h_t + h_r)² + L²) − sqrt((h_t − h_r)² + L²), or 2 h_t h_r / L if L ≫ h_t + h_r. Δφ = 2π Δd / λ. Γ from (sin ψ − X) / (sin ψ + X) with X horizontal or vertical as in the two-ray section; if ε is not loaded, use Γ = −1 only at low grazing (below a few degrees) and for horizontal polarization. One-way field factor F = 1 + Γ exp(−j Δφ), ignoring the small difference in ray length for amplitude. Radar power factor is |F|⁴. If the Rayleigh phase 4π σ_h sin ψ / λ is not small, shrink |Γ| or skip the coherent sum. If the specular point is masked, F = 1.
6. Do not multiply by the one-way two-ray path gain h² h² / L⁴. The free-space radar equation already contributes R⁻⁴. |F|⁴ is the only multipath factor passed on. In the small-angle region it behaves as R⁻⁴ by itself, so the product is R⁻⁸, which is intended.
7. Clutter, if the beam illuminates the surface in this dwell. Grazing angle at slant range R_g to the patch: sin ψ = (2 a_e h_r + h_r² − R_g²) / (2 a_e R_g), then subtract the DEM slope along the ray. σ0 = 10^(γ_dB / 10) sin ψ, with γ from the class table at 10 GHz plus 5 log10(f_GHz / 10). Cell area, pulse-limited: A = R_g θ_az (c / (2 B)) / cos ψ, where B is the waveform bandwidth (use τ only if the pulse is simple). σ_c = σ0 A. If the elevation footprint is shorter than the pulse footprint, switch to the beam-limited area R_g² θ_az θ_el / sin ψ. Pass σ_c to detection as the competing RCS in that cell. For sea, use γ = −40 dB at sea state 3 or −30 dB at sea state 5, and keep |F|⁴ on the target only. If a future Nathanson or NRL lookup replaces γ, drop the extra propagation on the clutter.
8. Rain, if a rate R_mm_h is stored. γ_R = k R_mm_h^α from P.838-3 at the radar frequency and polarization. L_rain = 2 γ_R D_km. Pass L_rain as a two-way loss. Weather RCS waits on the η(Z) constant still listed as a gap; until then a sim may pass Z = 200 R_mm_h^1.6 as a debug output and not as a fake σ_w.
9. Outputs consumed by detection, per dwell: L_diff (dB, two-way), |F|⁴ (linear), L_rain (dB, two-way), σ_c (m²). Visibility is the product of the power factors, not a boolean, except for the deep-shadow cull in step 4.

Refresh a fixed site’s azimuth skyline when the scenario loads, using the same bulge and the DEM, one ray per degree out to d_r plus a margin. Airborne skylines refresh on position, not on every pulse. Multipath and J(ν) stay per target because both heights move: a missile in the y-up frame changes h_t every frame, and the lobe condition h_t = λ R / (4 h_r) moves with it.

### Data products

- DEM elevations, projected metres, same datum as `position.y`.
- Class grid: sea, flatland, farmland, woods, urban, mountain, plus a sea-state integer defaulting to 3.
- Optional σ_h per class.
- Scalar k = 4/3, not a duct model.
- Optional rain rate in mm/h.
- Radar constants already expected elsewhere: frequency, polarization (H or V), θ_az, θ_el, bandwidth or pulse length, antenna height above the platform.

### Gaps

- η(Z) for weather RCS, complex sea permittivity, the NRL equation’s operator layout, the rounded-hill extra loss, and any Falcon BMS or DCS comparison are unresolved in the sections above and should not be invented inside this algorithm.
- The 40 dB cull and the 1° skyline bin are frame-budget choices. They are not ITU numbers.
